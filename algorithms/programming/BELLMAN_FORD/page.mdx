## The Failure of Dijkstra's Horizon
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the trap" />

Dijkstra assumes that any prefix of a shortest path must itself be a shortest path. A single negative edge shatters that foundation: a town settled early with a low score can later be undercut by a longer detour that gains time.

Standard curricula frame Bellman–Ford as a sluggish fallback that repeatedly relaxes every edge until nothing changes. That framing hides the real mathematical object.

The repeated passes are not crude brute force. They are successive iterations of a monotone contracting operator converging to its least fixed point.

## The Deflationary Operator
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the invariant" />

The state consists of a single vector $d \in (\mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\})^V$, pinned at $d(s)=0$. Between rounds, nothing else is stored.

The final vector satisfies Bellman's equation for the principle of optimality (Bellman 1958):

$$
d(s)=0, \qquad d(v)=\min_{(u,v)\in E}\bigl(d(u)+w(u,v)\bigr)\quad(v\neq s)
$$

Define the relaxation operator $T$ by testing every incoming road against the current estimate:

$$
T(d)(v)=\min\Bigl\{\,d(v),\; \min_{(u,v)\in E}\bigl(d(u)+w(u,v)\bigr)\Bigr\}, \qquad T(d)(s)=0
$$

Then $T(d)=d$ means no road can be relaxed, and one round of Bellman–Ford is one application of $T$. The operator possesses two essential properties:

- **Monotone.** If $d \le d'$ entrywise, then $T(d) \le T(d')$. Minimums and additions preserve order.
- **Deflationary.** $T(d) \le d$ for every vector $d$. Each step either reduces a distance estimate or leaves it unchanged; a label never rises.

Because $T$ is deflationary, distance estimates descend monotonically from $+\infty$ and can never fall beneath the true shortest paths. When a round produces no change, $T(d) = d$, certifying that the vector has reached its least fixed point.

## The Finite Horizon and the Negative Abyss
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the algebra" />

Start at $d^{(0)}$ with source at $0$ and all other entries at $+\infty$. The sequence $d^{(0)} \ge d^{(1)} \ge d^{(2)} \ge \cdots$ drops monotonically until it stabilizes.

**Theorem (Shimbel 1955; Bellman 1958).** If no negative cycle is reachable from source $s$, then $d^{(n-1)}$ is the exact shortest-path vector. Any subsequent round changes nothing.

The proof is a cycle-elimination argument. Any walk that visits more than $n-1$ edges must repeat a vertex, forming a cycle. Because no reachable cycle has negative weight, excising that cycle cannot increase total cost.

Therefore, an optimal walk without cycles exists, containing at most $n-1$ edges. Each application of $T$ extends optimal paths by at least one edge, guaranteeing convergence within $n-1$ passes.

### Tropical Matrix Powers

Let $L$ be the adjacency matrix with $L[u][v]=w(u,v)$ and $+\infty$ otherwise. Under tropical operations $\oplus=\min$ and $\otimes=+$, the $k$-th power measures exact path lengths:

$$
\bigl(L^{(k)}\bigr)[u][v]=\text{cheapest cost of a walk } u\to v \text{ with exactly } k \text{ roads}, \qquad L^{(0)}=I
$$

where $I$ has $0$ on the diagonal and $+\infty$ off it. One Bellman–Ford round is the tropical affine mapping $d \mapsto d \oplus (d \otimes L)$. After $k$ rounds, the vector matches row $s$ of the partial closure $\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k} L^{(j)}$.

The complete sum $L^{\star}=\bigoplus_{j\ge 0} L^{(j)}$ is the min-plus matrix closure. It satisfies $X = I \oplus (X \otimes L)$ and stabilizes at $j=n-1$ because simple paths have length at most $n-1$.

### The n-th Round: Detecting the Abyss

Run one round past $n-1$. The test asks if any road still satisfies $d^{(n-1)}(u) + w(u,v) < d^{(n-1)}(v)$.

If a label still drops, a walk with $n$ edges beats every shorter walk. Stripping cycles from that walk cannot increase weight unless the cycle itself has strictly negative weight (Ford 1956; Bellman 1958).

When a negative cycle is reachable, no finite fixed point exists. Each lap around the cycle subtracts energy, driving distances toward $-\infty$. The $n$-th round fires an alarm precisely because the fixed point has vanished.

Add road `T→P` with weight −10 to the picture. Now `P→R→T→P` is a cycle of total weight $3 - 4 - 10 = -11$, and every lap removes 11 from the labels it touches.

## Distance Vectors and Currency Arbitrage
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the simulation" />

Distance-vector routing protocols such as RIP implement Bellman–Ford across physical networks. Each router broadcasts its vector of distances to immediate neighbours, updating local routing tables according to the Bellman equation.

The infamous 'count-to-infinity' failure mode occurs when a link breaks and routers bounce obsolete distances back and forth. This is the physical realization of a missing fixed point, requiring split-horizon rules and hop-count limits.

Currency arbitrage is the exact inverse application. Set edge weights to $-\log(\text{exchange rate})$ so that multiplying currency conversions corresponds to summing weights. A profitable round trip becomes a negative cycle, transforming the $n$-th round test into an arbitrage detector.

## Moore's Queue: Chasing the Active Frontier

Relaxing all $E$ edges in every round costs $O(VE)$ operations. Moore (1959) introduced the Shortest Path Faster Algorithm (SPFA), keeping a queue of vertices whose distances recently decreased.

Only edges leaving active vertices are relaxed. While worst-case complexity remains $O(VE)$, typical execution speeds rival Dijkstra on sparse graphs.
