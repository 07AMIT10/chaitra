## Thirty questions to locate an atom
<ChapterMark minutes={3} tag="the hook" />

In 1986, Jon Bentley challenged professional programmers at Bell Labs and IBM to write binary search. Over ninety percent introduced off-by-one errors or infinite loops, despite having hours to test their work. The algorithm requires scarcely twenty lines of code, yet its precision punishes the slightest careless assumption.

At $10^{18}$ records, a scan from the left costs 31.7 years at its worst. The average is 15.8 years. Halving the interval needs 60 comparisons.

Eight sorted numbers sit in a row: `[3, 9, 14, 21, 27, 33, 41, 58]`. You want the 27.

Inspect index 3, see 21, and extinguish every index at or below 3 because sortedness forbids them from equaling 27. Inspect index 5, see 33, and the entire upper bracket vanishes. In two comparisons, seven candidates evaporate, leaving index 4 as the 27.

## The illusion of looking at the middle
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the trap" />

The standard operational framing is familiar: look at the midpoint, compare values, branch left or right, and repeat. That recipe tells you what to type, but obscures why the process runs so fast.

A naive linear search tests elements sequentially, spending up to $n$ comparisons. The fundamental question is not which element to inspect next. The true question is how much doubt a single comparison can extinguish.

## The invariant: contracting the horizon of doubt
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the invariant" />

The active state of binary search is an interval of surviving candidate indices, $[lo, hi)$. Outside this bracket, every index has been mathematically proven impossible:

$$
\left(\exists i : a_i = t\right) \implies \exists i \in [lo, hi) : a_i = t
$$

Sortedness is the contract that legitimizes mass elimination. If $a_{mid} < t$, then transitivity guarantees that $a_j \le a_{mid} < t$ for every $j \le mid$, allowing one comparison to discard an entire prefix.

The midpoint is merely the optimal halving point. What the algorithm maintains is an interval guaranteed to preserve the answer, while halving its length at every step.

## The exact arithmetic of thirty questions
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the algebra" />

A probe costs one comparison and leaves a subproblem at most half as large:

$$
T(n) = T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) + 1, \qquad T(1) = 1
$$

For powers of two, $n = 2^k$, the recurrence unrolls into a pure count of halvings:

$$
T(2^k) = T(2^{k-1}) + 1 = k + T(1) = k + 1
$$

Inverting back to $n$ yields an exact algebraic identity:

$$
T(n) = \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1 = \lceil \log_2 (n+1) \rceil
$$

This matches the balanced master theorem case with $a = 1$, $b = 2$, and $d = 0$, giving $\Theta(\log n)$.

Why is this optimal? Any comparison algorithm forms **the decision tree**: each internal node is a comparison, each leaf is a candidate, and $k$ two-way questions yield at most $2^k$ leaves. At $n = 10^{18}$ its depth is 60.

An array of size $n$ has $n + 1$ potential outcomes ($n$ indices plus "absent"). To distinguish every outcome, the tree must have at least $n+1$ distinct leaves:

$$
2^k \ge n + 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k \ge \lceil \log_2 (n+1) \rceil
$$

Binary search matches this information-theoretic lower bound exactly (Knuth 1973, §5.3.1).

## Monotone predicates: from git bisect to root finding
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="systems" />

Binary search generalizes far beyond sorted arrays. It applies to any monotone boolean predicate $P(x)$ that transitions from false to true:

- **`git bisect`.** Finds the exact regression commit by treating "does the bug reproduce here?" as a monotone predicate over chronological history.
- **Root finding by bisection.** Finds where a continuous function crosses zero by checking $\text{sign}(f(mid))$, halving the bracket interval each step.
- **Binary lifting.** Finds lowest common ancestors in trees in $O(\log n)$ by querying ancestor powers of two.

Breaking the comparison model breaks the logarithmic barrier. Interpolation search achieves $O(\log \log n)$ on uniform keys, while hash tables achieve $O(1)$ by forfeiting ordering entirely.

## The integer overflow bug that survived nine years in Java
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="frontier" />

Computing the midpoint as `mid = (lo + hi) / 2` contains a subtle flaw. When $lo + hi$ exceeds $2^{31} - 1$, 32-bit signed integer addition overflows into negative numbers, causing array index out-of-bounds exceptions.

This bug persisted unnoticed inside Java's `java.util.Arrays.binarySearch` for over nine years (Bloch 2006). The bug-free formulation is `mid = lo + (hi - lo) / 2` or `(lo + hi) >>> 1`.

Precision in endpoint updates is essential. An incorrect endpoint update either enters an infinite loop or skips the target entirely.
