## Karatsuba is not a faster way to multiply big numbers. It is the rank of polynomial multiplication dropping from four to three.

Multiply `1234` by `5678`. Cut each number in half at base $B = 100$, so $a_1 = 12$, $a_0 = 34$, $b_1 = 56$, $b_0 = 78$.

```
   schoolbook — four products              karatsuba — three products
     12 × 56 =   672  ->  B²                z₂ = 12 × 56            =  672
     12 × 78 =   936  ->  B                 z₀ = 34 × 78            = 2652
     34 × 56 =  1904  ->  B                 46 × 134               = 6164
     34 × 78 =  2652  ->  1                 z₁ = 6164 − 672 − 2652 = 2840

   672·B² + (936 + 1904)·B + 2652        672·B² + 2840·B + 2652
   = 7,006,652                           = 7,006,652
```

Both columns read `7,006,652`. The right column got there with three small multiplications instead of four, and paid for the difference with two additions and a subtraction.

At four digits that saves almost nothing. Apply it all the way down and it rewrites the exponent of multiplication.

## 1 · The question

The operational statement is the multiplication you learned at school. Line the numbers up, multiply every digit of one by every digit of the other, and add the shifted rows.

Two `n`-digit numbers meet in $n^2$ single-digit products. Turn that into a recursion by splitting each number in half and forming the four products $a_1 b_1$, $a_1 b_0$, $a_0 b_1$, $a_0 b_0$, and nothing improves: four subproblems of half the size is $T(n) = 4T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$, which the master theorem reads straight back as $\Theta(n^2)$.

So the operational framing is a schedule, and no schedule is the real question. The real question is how many of those products are *essential*. Additions, subtractions and shifts are linear, and linear work is the cheap work. The products are what a multiplier actually has to reduce.

## 2 · The real object: the multiplication tensor, and its rank

Write the two numbers as polynomials in the base: $x = \sum_i a_i B^i$ and $y = \sum_j b_j B^j$. Before any carrying, the digits of the product are the convolution

$$ c_k = \sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j $$

and the carries are a linear pass afterwards. So the only nonlinear work in multiplication is this convolution, a bilinear map in the two coefficient vectors.

A bilinear algorithm that uses `r` products is a decomposition of that map into `r` rank-1 terms. Each term is one product of a combination of the `a`'s with a combination of the `b`'s, $u_\ell(a)\cdot v_\ell(b)$, feeding into the output with its own weight $w_\ell(B)$.

Schoolbook's four products are four such terms, one per pair of blocks, so its rank is at most 4. Karatsuba's scheme is the same object with three:

$$ a_1 b_1\,(B^2 - B) \;+\; a_0 b_0\,(1 - B) \;+\; (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0)\,B $$

Multiply it out and the weights $B^2 - B$ and $1 - B$ are exactly what cancel the cross terms the single middle product swept up. The rank of two-block multiplication is 3.

The rank is the object. The exponent of the whole algorithm is how that rank compounds under recursion, and this is the framing Knuth gives the problem (*TAOCP* Vol. 2, §4.3.3, 1981). It is also the move Strassen makes in two dimensions, where the block-product rank drops from 8 to 7.

## 3 · The mathematics: derive the identity, then the recurrence, then the rank

**The identity, derived.** Take the split $x = a_1 B^m + a_0$ and $y = b_1 B^m + b_0$ and multiply out.

$$ (a_1 B^m + a_0)(b_1 B^m + b_0) \;=\; a_1 b_1 B^{2m} + (a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1)B^m + a_0 b_0 $$

The middle coefficient is the only awkward part: it is a sum of the two *cross* products. So compute the product of the two sums, which is what evaluating both factors at $1$ gives you.

$$ (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) \;=\; a_1 b_1 + a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1 + a_0 b_0 $$

Solve that for the cross pair,

$$ a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1 \;=\; (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) - a_1 b_1 - a_0 b_0 $$

and substitute it back. The two cross products are gone, replaced by one product of the sums and two subtractions:

$$ x\,y \;=\; z_2 B^{2m} + z_1 B^m + z_0, \qquad z_2 = a_1 b_1,\quad z_0 = a_0 b_0,\quad z_1 = (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) - z_2 - z_0 $$

Three products and four additions compute the same block product. Nothing was assumed about the base `B`, so the identity is exact over the integers, not an approximation (Karatsuba and Ofman 1962).

**The recurrence.** Each of $z_2$, $z_1$, $z_0$ is itself a product of half-size numbers, so call the method on each.

$$ T(n) \;=\; 3\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n) $$

The $\Theta(n)$ collects everything that is not a product: the sums $a_1 + a_0$ and $b_1 + b_0$, the two subtractions that recover $z_1$, and the shifts and adds that place the three pieces.

**The additions do not set the exponent.** At the top call the non-product work is proportional to `n`, a polynomial of degree `d = 1`. The products recurse into three calls of half the size, so `a = 3` and `b = 2`, and the master theorem compares $\log_b a = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ against `d = 1`. Since $\log_2 3 \gt 1$, the leaves dominate.

$$ T(n) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_2 3}\right) \;\approx\; \Theta\!\left(n^{1.585}\right) $$

The linear additions ride along as a constant factor. At every level the additive work is a fixed multiple of that level's product work, and the level totals grow by $3/2$ per level, so the deepest level holds the largest share and the additions join it rather than outweighing it. Push the additions to quadratic work (`d = 2`) and the root would dominate instead, forcing the exponent to `2` no matter what the rank is. (Master theorem, leaf case; Knuth 1981, §4.3.3.)

**The bilinear rank is exact: 2r − 1 for r blocks.** An `R`-product scheme writes every product of the map as $\sum_{\ell=1}^{R} u_\ell(a)\,v_\ell(b)\,w_\ell$, so all products lie in the span of the `R` weight vectors $w_\ell$. The products of two `r`-block numbers span all $2r - 1$ coefficients of their convolution, so `R` is at least $2r - 1$.

The bound is met. Evaluate both factors at $2r - 1$ points chosen so the evaluations need only additions, multiply the values pointwise for $2r - 1$ products, then interpolate; the interpolation is a linear solve. So the rank is exactly $2r - 1$, and at `r = 2` it is the 3 that Karatsuba used. Rank counts essential products and nothing else — it is silent about the additions, which is what the recurrence above has to add back in.

**Toom–Cook: split into r parts, pay 2r − 1 products.** Two blocks is the case `r = 2`. Split each number into `r` parts instead and the same evaluation recipe gives $2r - 1$ products per node (Toom 1963; Cook 1966).

$$ T(n) \;=\; (2r - 1)\,T(n/r) + \Theta(n), \qquad T(n) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_r (2r - 1)}\right) $$

The exponent falls as `r` grows: $\log_3 5 \approx 1.465$ for three parts, $\log_4 7 \approx 1.404$ for four, and since $(2r-1)/r$ tends to $2$ the exponent tends to `1`. The exponent is not the whole story. Each extra part adds a fixed cost — the evaluations, the interpolation, and divisions that must be exact — so the constant grows as the exponent falls.

**The FFT route: Schönhage–Strassen 1971.** Take the evaluation idea to its limit and evaluate at all the `n`-th roots of unity. A discrete Fourier transform evaluates all `n` points using $O(n \log n)$ additions and multiplications by constants (the twiddle factors), the pointwise multiplication is `n` products rather than $n^2$, and the inverse transform interpolates. The number of essential products becomes linear.

The obstacle is the ring. Roots of unity of enough orders exist in $\mathbb{Z}/(2^m+1)$ only for $m$ a power of two, so the operands are cut into blocks of about $\log n$ bits and each block is transformed separately. That block structure is the price of the method, and it is exactly the extra factor in Schönhage and Strassen's bound:

$$ M(n) \;=\; O(n \log n \log \log n) $$

**The honest lower bound.** You must read every digit, so the trivial lower bound is $\Omega(n)$. The best known algorithms sit close to $n \log n$ — Schönhage–Strassen at $n \log n \log \log n$, then Harvey and van der Hoeven's $O(n \log n)$ (2019, published 2021), which improved Fürer's $n \log n \cdot 2^{O(\log^* n)}$ (2007). Neither pins the answer down. Whether integer multiplication is $\Theta(n \log n)$, or lower, or needs another logarithmic factor, is open, and there is no general lower bound beyond $\Omega(n)$. Those two bounds are the honest state of the problem: the upper bound is near-linear, the matching lower bound is not known.

Read the ribbon top to bottom. Level `k` holds $3^k$ subproblems of size $n/2^k$, and each level's total is larger than the one above by the factor $3/2$, so the bars grow downward and the deepest level, the leaves, holds the most work. That is why the panel names the case *leaf-dominated* and prints $\Theta(n^{\log_2 3})$.

Now move `a`. Set it to 4 and the same panel draws schoolbook: the ribbon grows by $4/2 = 2$ per level, the leaves dominate harder, and the read-out becomes $\Theta(n^{\log_2 4}) = \Theta(n^2)$. The two settings for `a` are the two algorithms, side by side, differing only in the rank of their node.

Then move `d`. At `d = 2` the additions are quadratic, $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ falls below `d = 2`, the *root* dominates, and the exponent is `2` whatever the rank is. The additions are linear in this problem, and that is what lets the rank show through the panel.

## 5 · The payoff

The rank is the whole trade, and the trade is everywhere.

- **Every big-integer library.** CPython's large `int` product, Java's `BigInteger`, and GMP all leave the schoolbook loop for Karatsuba above a few dozen digits, then chain Toom-3 and an FFT method as the operands grow. RSA and Diffie–Hellman are chains of exactly these products.
- **The same rank move, two indices wide.** Strassen's algorithm (1969) multiplies two 2×2 blocks with 7 products instead of 8, and recursion gives $\Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx \Theta(n^{2.807})$ against schoolbook's $\Theta(n^3)$. Karatsuba is the one-index case of that idea.
- **Polynomial multiplication, unchanged.** The convolution is the same bilinear map, so every method here applies to polynomials directly; FFT multiplication is the standard tool, and Kronecker substitution encodes a polynomial product as one big-integer product.
- **A counting rule.** When multiplication is the expensive operation and addition is cheap, count the essential products — the rank — and recurse. That rule is the entry point to algebraic complexity.

## 6 · Advanced and optional

Skip this; nothing below is used again.

**The constant, not the exponent, picks the algorithm in practice.** Karatsuba's per-call overhead is larger than schoolbook's, so it wins only above a crossover size; the same is true between Karatsuba and Toom-3, and again before an FFT method pays. A production library is a chain of these, each chosen by operand size, and the crossover points are found by measurement, not by the exponents.

**The general question is open.** For integer multiplication the best known upper bound is $O(n \log n)$ (Harvey and van der Hoeven 2019/2021) and the best general lower bound is still $\Omega(n)$; nothing known places the true complexity, and no one rules out $\Theta(n \log n)$ or a bound below it. Matrix multiplication has the same gap: the best known exponent is still above 2.37, and $\omega = 2$ is conjectured, not known.

**Bilinear rank is pinned down; bit complexity is not.** The bilinear rank of the `r`-block product is exactly $2r - 1$ by the counting argument and the evaluation scheme above. That is the rank of the non-recursive block map. What stays open is the *integer* problem: there the base is an integer rather than a field, additions and carries are not free, and the interpolating divisions must be exact, so the rank alone does not determine the cost.

- **Karatsuba and Ofman 1960/1962** — the three-product identity and the $\Theta(n^{\log_2 3})$ recursion. Karatsuba found it in 1960 after Kolmogorov conjectured that $n^2$ multiplications were necessary; it was announced at Kolmogorov's seminar and published in *Doklady Akademii Nauk SSSR* in 1962.
- **Toom 1963** — the split into `r` parts and the $2r - 1$ pointwise products, the generalisation that lowers the exponent toward 1.
- **Cook 1966** — the same scheme as an algorithm on a random-access machine, with the interpolation made explicit; source of the name Toom–Cook.
- **Schönhage and Strassen 1971** — the FFT algorithm, $O(n \log n \log \log n)$, the first method to bring the exponent to essentially 1 up to a log log n factor.
- **Knuth 1981** — *The Art of Computer Programming*, Vol. 2, §4.3.3, where integer multiplication is treated as a bilinear map and the rank framing is developed.
- **Strassen 1969** — the two-index version, rank 7 for 2×2 blocks; used on this page only as the analogue that names its thesis.
- **Fürer 2007** — $n \log n \cdot 2^{O(\log^* n)}$, the first algorithm to beat Schönhage–Strassen's log log n factor over a general ring.
- **Harvey and van der Hoeven 2019/2021** — the $O(n \log n)$ upper bound, the current best known for integer multiplication.
