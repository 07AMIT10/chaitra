## The student who disproved Kolmogorov

In 1960, Kolmogorov told a Moscow State University seminar that multiplying two $n$-digit numbers requires $\Omega(n^2)$ operations; the claim lasted one week. A 23-year-old student, Anatoly Karatsuba, ended it with an identity that uses three half-size products instead of four.

At $n = 2^{20}$, four half-size products per split leave $4^{20} = 1.1\times10^{12}$ leaf products. Three leave $3^{20} = 3.49\times10^{9}$. That is 315× fewer multiplications.

Multiply `1234` by `5678`. Cut each number in half at base $B = 100$, so $a_1 = 12$, $a_0 = 34$, $b_1 = 56$, $b_0 = 78$.

```
   schoolbook — four products              karatsuba — three products
     12 × 56 =   672  ->  B²                z₂ = 12 × 56            =  672
     12 × 78 =   936  ->  B                 z₀ = 34 × 78            = 2652
     34 × 56 =  1904  ->  B                 46 × 134               = 6164
     34 × 78 =  2652  ->  1                 z₁ = 6164 − 672 − 2652 = 2840

   672·B² + (936 + 1904)·B + 2652        672·B² + 2840·B + 2652
   = 7,006,652                           = 7,006,652
```

Both columns produce `7,006,652`. The right column arrives with three small multiplications instead of four, paying for the saving with simple additions and subtractions.

The picture is **three shared measurements**: $a_1b_1$, $a_0b_0$, and $(a_1+a_0)(b_1+b_0)$. The middle measurement carries both cross terms, and subtracting the two squares leaves $a_1b_0 + a_0b_1$.

At four digits, that saving looks modest. Applied recursively to million-digit numbers, it transforms the computational universe.

## The quadratic illusion of grade school

Grade school teaches us to multiply digit by digit: line up the rows, compute $n^2$ single-digit products, and sum the shifted rows.

If you split each $n$-digit number into two halves and compute the four products $a_1 b_1$, $a_1 b_0$, $a_0 b_1$, $a_0 b_0$, nothing improves. Four subproblems of half the size yield $T(n) = 4T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$, which the master theorem reads right back as $\Theta(n^2)$.

Loop unrolling, SIMD registers, and cache blocking change the constant factor, but cannot break the quadratic wall. The true bottleneck is not the schedule of additions, but the number of essential scalar multiplications.

## The algebraic engine: dropping tensor rank from 4 to 3

Write the two numbers as polynomials in the radix base: $x = \sum_i a_i B^i$ and $y = \sum_j b_j B^j$. Before carry propagation, the digits of the product form a discrete convolution:

$$
c_k = \sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j
$$

Carries are resolved in a single linear pass afterwards. The only nonlinear operation in multiplication is this bilinear map of coefficient vectors.

A bilinear algorithm using $r$ multiplications decomposes that map into $r$ rank-1 terms. Schoolbook multiplication evaluates four terms, establishing a rank of 4. Karatsuba rewrites the exact same polynomial product using only three rank-1 terms:

$$
a_1 b_1\,(B^2 - B) \;+\; a_0 b_0\,(1 - B) \;+\; (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0)\,B
$$

Multiply it out: the weights $B^2 - B$ and $1 - B$ cancel the unwanted cross terms gathered by the middle product. The algebraic rank of two-block polynomial multiplication is 3, not 4.

## Deriving the identity and riding the recurrence

Take the split $x = a_1 B^m + a_0$ and $y = b_1 B^m + b_0$, and expand their product:

$$
(a_1 B^m + a_0)(b_1 B^m + b_0) \;=\; a_1 b_1 B^{2m} + (a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1)B^m + a_0 b_0
$$

The sum of cross terms $a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1$ appears to require two multiplications. Karatsuba evaluates the product of the sums instead:

$$
(a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) \;=\; a_1 b_1 + a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1 + a_0 b_0
$$

Solve directly for the cross pair by subtracting the outer products:

$$
a_1 b_0 + a_0 b_1 \;=\; (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) - a_1 b_1 - a_0 b_0
$$

Substitute this back to evaluate the full product with three multiplications:

$$
x\,y \;=\; z_2 B^{2m} + z_1 B^m + z_0, \qquad z_2 = a_1 b_1,\quad z_0 = a_0 b_0,\quad z_1 = (a_1 + a_0)(b_1 + b_0) - z_2 - z_0
$$

Because $z_2$, $z_1$, and $z_0$ are each products of half-size integers, we recurse on all three:

$$
T(n) \;=\; 3\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n)
$$

The master theorem compares $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ against the linear overhead degree $d = 1$. Because $\log_2 3 > 1$, the recursion tree is leaf-dominated:

$$
T(n) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_2 3}\right) \;\approx\; \Theta\!\left(n^{1.585}\right)
$$

## From CPython to RSA: where the trick lives

Karatsuba is active code running on billions of machines daily.

In CPython, Python's arbitrary-precision `int` type switches from schoolbook multiplication to Karatsuba as soon as integers exceed 70 digits. The GNU Multiple Precision Arithmetic Library (GMP) and Java's `BigInteger` rely on it across similar ranges. Every RSA key generation and Diffie-Hellman modular exponentiation executes chains of Karatsuba multiplications.

The same algebraic principle applies identically to polynomial multiplication. Polynomial convolution in computer algebra systems evaluates using Karatsuba before switching to fast Fourier transforms.

## Beyond Karatsuba: Toom-Cook, FFTs, and the open frontier

Two-block splitting is only the first step. Splitting each operand into $r$ parts yields the Toom-Cook family of algorithms (Toom 1963, Cook 1966).

$$
T(n) \;=\; (2r - 1)\,T(n/r) + \Theta(n), \qquad T(n) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_r (2r - 1)}\right)
$$

Taking polynomial evaluation to the limit gives the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Evaluating polynomials at roots of unity drops multiplication to near-linear time:

$$
M(n) \;=\; O(n \log n \log \log n)
$$

Schönhage and Strassen (1971) established this bound over Fermat rings. In 2019, David Harvey and Joris van der Hoeven proved that $n$-digit integers can be multiplied in $O(n \log n)$ time.

Whether $O(n \log n)$ is the ultimate lower bound remains one of the great open questions in theoretical computer science. The only universal lower bound known is $\Omega(n)$—the time required simply to read the digits.

## Historical provenance and attributions

- **Karatsuba and Ofman 1960/1962** — *Multiplication of Many-Digital Numbers by Automata*: discovered the 3-product identity in 1960, refuting Kolmogorov's conjecture; published in 1962.
- **Toom 1963** — *The Complexity of a Scheme of Functional Elements Realizing the Multiplication of Integers*: generalized the evaluation-interpolation technique to $r$ blocks.
- **Cook 1966** — *On the Minimum Computation Time of Functions*: formalized Toom's technique for random-access machines.
- **Schönhage and Strassen 1971** — *Fast Multiplication of Large Numbers*: introduced the FFT multiplication algorithm over modular rings, achieving $O(n \log n \log \log n)$.
- **Knuth 1981** — *The Art of Computer Programming*, Vol. 2, §4.3.3: classic exposition framing integer multiplication through multilinear rank.
- **Harvey and van der Hoeven 2019/2021** — *Integer multiplication in time O(n log n)*: achieved the celebrated $O(n \log n)$ bound.
