## Maximum flow is not "push until you get stuck". It is a linear program whose dual is a cut, and the gap between them closes to exactly zero.

Five junctions sit on a table, labelled `s`, `a`, `b`, `c`, `t`, joined by seven one-way pipes. Each pipe carries an integer capacity: `s→a` holds 2, `s→b` holds 6, `a→b` holds 2, `a→c` holds 3, `b→c` holds 2, `c→t` holds 7, and `b→t` holds 3. Water enters at `s`, leaves at `t`, and never pools at `a`, `b` or `c`.

That is the whole artefact, and it already asks this page's question. What is the largest rate of water that can cross from `s` to `t`, and what object could certify that no larger rate exists?

## 1 · The question

The obvious framing is operational: push water along any route that still has room, and stop when you run out of routes. That procedure reaches a flow, but its stop condition is a statement about the routes you happened to try, not about the network.

Spare room is also not only forward room. A pipe already carrying three units hides three units of reverse capacity: cancelling that commitment reroutes water, at no cost, to a better route. The graph you must search is therefore the residual graph, and it is not just the seven pipes redrawn.

The deeper problem is that "get stuck" is a search, and the real question is an upper bound. To claim that 7 is best you must exhibit something that forbids 8, and no amount of pushing exhibits that. The bound has to be a second object.

## 2 · The real object: a flow, and the cut that bounds it

The state is a **feasible flow**: one number $f_e$ per pipe, with $0 \le f_e \le c_e$, and conservation at every junction except `s` and `t` — what enters equals what leaves. These 7-tuples form a convex polytope, and the flow value is a linear function on it:

$$ v(f) \;=\; \sum_{e \in \delta^+(s)} f_e $$

Write $\delta^+(S)$ for the pipes whose tail is in a set $S$ of junctions and whose head is not; the dashboard paints exactly those gold once a cut is tight.

The bounding object is a **cut**: a set $S$ of junctions with `s` in $S$ and `t` outside it, and its capacity $\operatorname{cap}(S) = \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} c_e$. A flow of value $v$ and a cut of capacity $c$ with $v = c$ settle the question completely, because the next section proves $v \le c$ always.

## 3 · The mathematics

### The program

Maximise the flow value subject to conservation and capacity:

$$ \max\; v \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{e \in \delta^+(u)} f_e - \sum_{e \in \delta^-(u)} f_e = \begin{cases} v & u = s, \\ -v & u = t, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \qquad 0 \le f_e \le c_e. $$

Every row of that system is a junction, every column is a pipe, and the variable $v$ is the quantity you want. This is the standard linear-programming form of max flow, and it is the object the rest of the page manipulates.

### Weak duality: the gap can never go negative

Take any feasible flow and any cut $S$, and add up conservation over the junctions inside $S$. The pipes internal to $S$ cancel, leaving one line of arithmetic:

$$ v(f) \;=\; \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} f_e \;-\; \sum_{e \in \delta^-(S)} f_e \;\le\; \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} f_e \;\le\; \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} c_e \;=\; \operatorname{cap}(S). $$

This is a derived identity, not a cited theorem, and it says the flow value can never exceed the cut capacity. On screen it is the top two cards: the flow card approaches the cut card only from below. Watch the third card, the gap; it starts at 8 and is non-negative at every step, because that is what the identity says.

### The dual is a cut

Give each junction a real potential $\pi_u$ and each pipe a price $z_e \ge 0$, with $\pi_s - \pi_t = 1$. The linear-programming dual of the program above is:

$$ \min \sum_e c_e z_e \quad \text{subject to} \quad z_e \ge \pi_u - \pi_v \ \text{for every pipe } e = (u,v). $$

At an optimum $z_e = \max(0, \pi_u - \pi_v)$; this is a short derivation, not a citation. Since $\max(0, a-b)$ measures the length of the interval from $b$ to $a$, the objective is the integral, over thresholds $t$ in the unit interval, of the capacity of the set $S_t = \{u : \pi_u \ge t\}$.

Each $S_t$ is an honest cut, because $\pi_s = 1 \ge t$ and $\pi_t = 0 \lt t$ put `s` inside it and `t` outside. So the dual is at least the smallest cut capacity, and the indicator $\pi = \mathbf{1}_S$ of a minimum cut attains that value. The dual is therefore exactly the minimum cut capacity, with no slack anywhere.

### The theorem, and the cut that falls out of the flow

**Theorem** (Ford–Fulkerson 1956; independently Elias–Feinstein–Shannon 1956): the maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity. The proof is the residual argument, and it builds the cut from the flow rather than searching for it.

For a pipe carrying $f_e$, the residual graph holds a forward arc of capacity $c_e - f_e$ and a backward arc of capacity $f_e$. An augmenting path is an `s`-to-`t` path in that graph, and pushing its bottleneck along it keeps every constraint true while raising $v$.

Suppose no augmenting path remains, and let $S$ be the set of junctions reachable from `s` in the residual graph. Every pipe leaving $S$ is saturated, or its forward residual arc would leave $S$; every pipe entering $S$ is empty, or its backward residual arc would enter $S$. Therefore

$$ v(f) = \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} f_e = \sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} c_e = \operatorname{cap}(S), $$

so this flow and this cut are optimal at the same instant. That is exactly the last step of the dashboard: the residual graph empties of `s`-to-`t` paths, the junctions split into `{s, b}` and `{a, c, t}`, and the table's cut rows light up.

### Integrality: why the answer is a whole number

If every capacity is an integer, an integral best flow exists, so a plumbing answer needs no rounding. The reason is the network matrix: its rows are junctions, its columns are pipes, and each column has one $+1$ and one $-1$. Such a matrix is **totally unimodular**, meaning every square submatrix has determinant 0 or $\pm 1$, and a totally unimodular matrix with an integral right-hand side has integral vertices (Hoffman–Kruskal 1956). So an integral-capacity network has an integral optimum, visible as the integers in every row of the dashboard table.

### The algorithm, and the cost of choosing badly

Plain Ford–Fulkerson (1956) augments along any residual path and stops when none remains; the residual argument shows that the flow it stops on is maximum. Its work, however, is not controlled by the size of the graph. Send the wrong path first and the number of augmentations can grow with the capacities themselves, not with the number of junctions.

The diamond has pipes `s→a` and `s→b` of capacity $C$, pipes `a→t` and `b→t` of capacity $C$, and one pipe `a→b` of capacity 1. Choosing `s→a→b→t` and then `s→b→a→t` moves one unit of value per pair of steps, so a bad choice costs $2C$ augmentations while the shortest-path rule costs two.

Edmonds and Karp (1972) removed the danger by always augmenting along a shortest path, found by breadth-first search. The number of augmentations is then $O(VE)$, and each search costs $O(E)$, for a total of $O(VE^2)$. Those are the paths the dashboard steps, so the run you drive is Edmonds–Karp, not plain Ford–Fulkerson.

The failure of the plain method is not a technicality. With irrational capacities it may never terminate (Ford–Fulkerson 1962), and the smallest network on which it stalls forever is due to Zwick (1995).

## 5 · The payoff

Max-flow/min-cut is the prototype of a min-max theorem, and the useful half is the dual certificate. To prove a flow is best you exhibit a cut of equal capacity, so optimality is a *witness* rather than a search that returned empty.

Feed every left vertex from a new source, drain every right vertex into a new sink, set all capacities to 1, and max flow becomes maximum bipartite matching; the minimum cut then certifies an optimal vertex cover (König 1931, Hall 1935). Menger's theorem (1927) is the same statement with unit pipes: the maximum number of edge-disjoint `s`-to-`t` paths equals the minimum cut. Both are this one linear program wearing different clothes.

Treat pixels as junctions and pixel similarities as capacities, and a minimum cut is an image segmentation, solved by max flow in practice (Boykov–Kolmogorov 2004). The pattern — a primal allocation meeting a dual scarce resource — is also the transportation and assignment problem (Hitchcock 1941; Kuhn 1955).

The habit worth keeping: when a combinatorial statement says "maximum equals minimum", look for the linear program and read off its dual. The dual is the thing that proves the maximum is a maximum.

## 6 · Advanced / optional (skippable)

You can stop here. What follows is for the reader who wants the general form and the neighbouring methods.

- **Hoffman's circulation theorem** (1960) is the feasibility form of this duality: a circulation with bounds $\ell_e \le f_e \le u_e$ and conservation at every junction exists exactly when $\sum_{e \in \delta^+(S)} \ell_e \le \sum_{e \in \delta^-(S)} u_e$ for every set $S$. Max flow is the special case with one extra pipe `t→s` whose lower bound is the value you want.
- **Complementary slackness** is the cut you can see: at an optimum, every pipe leaving the chosen $S$ carries exactly its capacity and every pipe entering $S$ carries zero. That is why the last frame's cut pipes are precisely the saturated ones.
- **Faster algorithms** replace plain augmenting paths. Dinic (1970) augments a whole level graph at once, and push–relabel (Goldberg–Tarjan 1988) reaches $O(V^2 \sqrt{E})$ for unit capacities. Total unimodularity itself has a signing characterisation: a matrix is totally unimodular exactly when every subset of its rows can be signed so that each column sums to a value in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ (Ghouila-Houri 1962).
