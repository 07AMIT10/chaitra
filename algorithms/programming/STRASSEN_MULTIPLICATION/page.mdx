## Seven products that broke the cubic wall

At $n = 2^{30}$, eight scalar products per $2\times2$ block leave $8^{30} = 1.24\times10^{27}$ leaf products. Seven leave $7^{30} = 2.25\times10^{25}$, a 55× saving for the same answer.

In 1968, Volker Strassen set out to prove that Gaussian elimination was optimal. Calculating by hand in Frankfurt, he found seven equations that shattered that assumption instead.

Take two $2\times 2$ matrices and multiply them both ways.

```
A = [ 1  2 ]      B = [ 5  6 ]
    [ 3  4 ]          [ 7  8 ]

textbook    a·e+b·g   a·f+b·h        eight products: ae bg af bh ce dg cf dh
            c·e+d·g   c·f+d·h        C = [ 19  22 ]
                                         [ 43  50 ]

Strassen    M1 = (1+4)(5+8)  =  65      c11 = M1 + M4 - M5 + M7 = 65 + 8 - 24 - 30 = 19
            M2 = (3+4)·5     =  35      c12 = M3 + M5           = -2 + 24        = 22
            M3 = 1·(6-8)    =  -2      c21 = M2 + M4           = 35 + 8         = 43
            M4 = 4·(7-5)    =   8      c22 = M1 - M2 + M3 + M6 = 65 - 35 - 2 + 22 = 50
            M5 = (1+2)·8    =  24
            M6 = (3-1)(5+6) =  22
            M7 = (2-4)(7+8) = -30
```

Count the multiplication signs. The schoolbook recipe demands eight scalar products; Strassen finds every entry of the output with seven. No intermediate product is recycled from the schoolbook list—every single term is an exotic combination.

The picture is **the seven measurements**: seven compound products $M_1$ through $M_7$, four outputs $C_{11}$ through $C_{22}$, and a sign wherever a measurement is added or removed.

## The fallacy of the loop reorder

The schoolbook algorithm is defined by the triple loop: `c[i][j] += a[i][k] * b[k][j]` across all `i`, `j`, `k`. It spends $n^3$ multiplications and $n^3 - n^2$ additions.

Programmers often suspect loop reordering, cache blocking, or vectorization could lower the multiplication count. But reordering terms can never change their quantity: each of the eight index triples $(i,k,j)$ still demands an independent product $a_{ik}\,b_{kj}$.

To beat $n^3$, you must abandon the requirement that factors are individual matrix entries. The true question permits factors to be *any* linear combinations of entries, asking for the minimum number of products whose linear combination yields $C$.

## Bilinear maps and the hidden tensor rank

Fix a field $\mathbb{F}$ and a size $n$. Matrix multiplication is the bilinear map:

$$
\mu : \mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\times\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}, \qquad \mu(A,B)=AB
$$

Bilinear means linear in each argument separately. Every bilinear map can be written as a **structure tensor**, an array of coefficients describing how each monomial $a_{ik}b_{kj}$ contributes to each output entry $c_{ij}$.

For matrix multiplication, the tensor is a sum of $n^3$ rank-one components:

$$
T_{nnn} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} e_i \otimes e_k \otimes e_j
$$

At $n=2$, this sum has eight terms, representing the textbook algorithm. The **tensor rank** $R(T)$ is the fewest rank-one tensors needed to sum to $T$:

$$
T \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{r} u_s \otimes v_s \otimes w_s, \qquad R(T) = \min r
$$

Vector $u_s$ forms a linear combination of entries from $A$, $v_s$ from $B$, and $w_s$ directs where their product lands in $C$. Thus, an $r$-term decomposition is an algorithm using $r$ scalar multiplications. The minimum multiplication count is identical to tensor rank.

## The seven identities and the Winograd floor

For general $2\times 2$ blocks, Strassen's seven products are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_1 &= (a_{11}+a_{22})(b_{11}+b_{22}), & M_2 &= (a_{21}+a_{22})\,b_{11}, & M_3 &= a_{11}(b_{12}-b_{22}),\\
M_4 &= a_{22}(b_{21}-b_{11}), & M_5 &= (a_{11}+a_{12})\,b_{22}, & M_6 &= (a_{21}-a_{11})(b_{11}+b_{12}),\\
M_7 &= (a_{12}-a_{22})(b_{21}+b_{22})
\end{aligned}
$$

The four output entries recombine these products using additions and subtractions:

$$
c_{11}=M_1+M_4-M_5+M_7, \quad c_{12}=M_3+M_5, \quad c_{21}=M_2+M_4, \quad c_{22}=M_1-M_2+M_3+M_6
$$

Expand $c_{22}=M_1-M_2+M_3+M_6$ and watch the unwanted cross-terms evaporate: $a_{11}b_{11}$, $a_{22}b_{11}$, $a_{11}b_{22}$, and $a_{21}b_{11}$ all cancel out in pairs. What survives is $a_{21}b_{12} + a_{22}b_{22}$, the exact inner product required.

Winograd (1971) proved that seven products are an absolute mathematical floor. No bilinear algorithm can multiply two $2\times 2$ matrices with six products. Therefore, $R(T_{222}) = 7$ exactly.

Because tensor rank is submultiplicative ($R(T \otimes T') \le R(T)R(T')$), recursing on half-size blocks yields:

$$
R\bigl(T_{222}^{\otimes k}\bigr) \;\le\; 7^k \;=\; n^{\log_2 7}
$$

The recurrence relation is:

$$
T(n) \;=\; 7\,T(n/2) \;+\; \Theta(n^2)
$$

Since $\log_2 7 \approx 2.807 > 2$, the master theorem places this firmly in the leaf-dominated case:

$$
T(n) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_2 7}\right) \;\approx\; \Theta\!\left(n^{2.807}\right)
$$

Drag the $a$-slider from 7 to 8. The printed asymptotic jumps from $\Theta(n^{2.807})$ straight to $\Theta(n^3)$. That single tick of the slider captures the whole gulf between Strassen and three centuries of matrix algebra.

Now slide $a$ down to 4. Because $\log_2 4 = 2$ matches $d=2$, every level does equal work, yielding $\Theta(n^2 \log n)$.

## The price of speed: precision and practical crossover

Speed is not free. Strassen's algorithm introduces numerical subtraction of large, nearly equal intermediate terms.

Over exact algebraic structures—like finite fields in cryptography or modular arithmetic in computer algebra—rounding errors do not exist. In those domains, the rank drop is pure gain.

Every modern high-performance implementation runs a hybrid: recurse using Strassen for massive blocks, then transition to classical cubic GEMM once blocks fit inside L1/L2 cache.

## The unfinished race for omega

Matrix multiplication is the universal engine of computational mathematics. Inversion, determinants, and LUP decomposition all share the exact same asymptotic complexity exponent $\omega$:

$$
\omega \;=\; \inf\{\, \tau \;:\; \text{two } n\times n \text{ matrices can be multiplied in } O(n^{\tau}) \text{ operations} \,\}
$$

The trivial boundaries are $2 \le \omega \le 3$: reading the inputs requires $n^2$ steps, while the textbook loop takes $n^3$. Strassen proved $\omega \le \log_2 7 \approx 2.807$.

For fifty years, mathematicians have hunted for lower-rank tensor decompositions of larger blocks. Coppersmith and Winograd (1990) used the laser method on arithmetic progressions to prove $\omega < 2.376$. Alman and Williams (2021) trimmed this to $\omega < 2.37286$.

Yet those modern constructions are purely galactic: their hidden constants are so colossal that they would only surpass Strassen on matrices larger than the observable universe. Strassen's algorithm remains the only sub-cubic method fast enough to run on real hardware.

## Historical provenance and attributions

- **Strassen 1969** — *Gaussian elimination is not optimal*: introduced the 7-product identity, established $O(n^{\log_2 7})$, and reduced inversion and determinants to matrix multiplication.
- **Winograd 1971** — *On multiplication of 2×2 matrices*: proved $R(T_{222}) = 7$ is minimal, and constructed the 15-addition variant.
- **Coppersmith and Winograd 1990** — *Matrix multiplication via arithmetic progressions*: proved $\omega < 2.376$, standing as the frontier for two decades.
- **Alman and Williams 2021** — *A refined laser method and faster matrix multiplication*: advanced the theoretical bound to $\omega < 2.37286$.
- **Higham 2002** — *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, §23.2: authoritative numerical stability analysis of Strassen's recurrence.
- **Huang, Smith, Henry and van de Geijn 2016** — *Strassen's Algorithm for Matrix Multiplication*: empirical benchmarks pinning down practical crossover points on contemporary architectures.
