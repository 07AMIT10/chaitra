## The Greedy Myopia

Suppose you walk down the roster in alphabetical order, assigning each applicant the first available role they accept.

Ana acts first and claims Wren. Next arrives Ben: because Ben qualifies only for Wren, Ben is stranded without an assignment, even though Xiu and Zed sit entirely vacant!

A rigid algorithm that never revokes a handshake permanently stalls at 2 matches. True optimization requires the ability to reach backwards, shift existing commitments, and reopen pathways for stranded peers.

## Alternating Paths and Edge Independence

A **matching** is a subset of edges sharing no common endpoints. Edges in the matching are active bonds; all remaining edges are idle candidates. A vertex touching no active edge is **free**.

An **alternating path** weaves between non-matched and matched edges in strict alternation. If such a path starts and ends at free vertices, it is an **augmenting path**.

Flipping the chain of resignations transforms every non-matched link into an active bond and demotes every matched link. Because the path began and ended on free edges, the total matching size increases by exactly one!

## The Triad of Equivalence: Hall, Berge, and König

Three classical theorems pin the exact limits of bipartite matching.

**Hall's Marriage Theorem (Hall 1935).** A bipartite graph admits a matching covering every left vertex if and only if every left subset $S$ satisfies $|N(S)| \ge |S|$. On our board, the subset $S = \{\text{Cara}, \text{Dan}\}$ has $N(S) = \{\text{Yara}\}$, proving algebraically why 4 matches are impossible.

**Berge's Lemma (Claude Berge 1957).** A matching is maximum if and only if it admits zero augmenting paths. If any larger matching exists, their symmetric difference must contain an alternating path with more edges from the superior matching, which is by definition an augmenting path. When augmenting path search returns empty, optimality is guaranteed.

**König's Duality Theorem (Dénes König 1931).** In any bipartite graph, the size of a maximum matching equals the size of a minimum vertex cover:

$$
\text{maximum matching size} = \text{minimum vertex cover size} \qquad \text{(bipartite graphs only)}
$$

On our board, the vertex subset $\{\text{Ana}, \text{Ben}, \text{Yara}\}$ contains 3 vertices and intercepts every single permitted edge. Since this cover has size 3 and our matching achieves size 3, weak duality snaps shut with zero gap.

The bipartite restriction is indispensable: an odd cycle like a triangle has maximum matching 1, but demands 2 vertices to cover all edges.

## Timetables, Corridors, and the Hopcroft–Karp Squeeze

Bipartite matching models resource arbitration across industry: assigning medical shifts, scheduling examination timetables, and matching display advertisers to viewer impressions.

When graphs scale to millions of nodes, searching for single augmenting paths takes $O(V E)$ time. Hopcroft & Karp (1973) accelerated this by structuring execution into phases: each phase constructs a BFS level graph and extracts a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths in parallel.

Because each phase strictly increases the length of shortest augmenting paths, at most $O(\sqrt{V})$ phases are required, yielding an overall runtime of:

$$
O(E\sqrt{V}) \qquad \text{(Hopcroft–Karp 1973)}
$$

## The Birkhoff Polytope and the 0–1 Permanent

To compute the total count of distinct perfect matchings rather than locating just one, inspect the 0–1 biadjacency matrix $A$. The number of perfect matchings is the **permanent** of $A$—the signless analog of the determinant.

Leslie Valiant (1979) proved computing the permanent is #P-complete, demonstrating the vast gulf between finding one witness and counting all witnesses.

The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem (Birkhoff 1946; von Neumann 1953) illuminates the geometry: the convex hull of all permutation matrices forms the Birkhoff polytope of doubly stochastic matrices. Fractional matchings form a continuous polyhedron whose extreme vertices are precisely the discrete matchings.
