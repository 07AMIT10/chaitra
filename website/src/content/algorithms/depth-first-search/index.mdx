## Beyond the Cursor's Trail

Ask a programmer what depth-first search does and you will hear a mechanical rule: dive as deep as possible, then backtrack. Ask what the algorithm produces and you will receive the sequence of visited vertices.

That sequence is an arbitrary artefact of input ordering and dictionary sorting. Reordering adjacent arcs completely scrambles the visit sequence while leaving the underlying graph topology untouched.

The algorithm's true output is not a path. It is a spanning forest decorated with two timestamps per vertex that encode the global connectivity of the network.

## The Laminar World: Nested Time Intervals

Strip the traversal of commentary and a rooted spanning forest remains. Every vertex except a tree root has a unique parent that discovered it, linked by tree edges.

Assign each vertex two distinct timestamps. Let $d[v]$ be the tick when the search enters $v$, and let $f[v]$ be the tick when its subroutine returns. The clock advances on every event, allocating ticks 1 through 12 across the six vertices.

| vertex | enter $d[v]$ | leave $f[v]$ | interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1 | 12 | 1 to 12 |
| B | 2 | 7 | 2 to 7 |
| D | 3 | 6 | 3 to 6 |
| E | 4 | 5 | 4 to 5 |
| C | 8 | 11 | 8 to 11 |
| F | 9 | 10 | 9 to 10 |

Each vertex owns an interval $[d[v], f[v]]$. This family of intervals is strictly **laminar**: any two intervals are either nested or completely disjoint, never partially overlapping. That single structural invariance drives every theorem on this page.

## The Parenthesis Theorem and Arc Taxonomy

### The Parenthesis Invariant

$$
u \ne v \implies [d_u, f_u] \subseteq [d_v, f_v] \text{ or } [d_v, f_v] \subseteq [d_u, f_u] \text{ or } [d_u, f_u] \cap [d_v, f_v] = \varnothing
$$

This is the **parenthesis theorem** (Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, and Stein). A single call stack permits no third state: subroutine calls are entered and exited in strictly nested order.

### The Taxonomy of Four Arc Types

Every directed arc $(u, v)$ is categorized the instant its tail $u$ inspects it:

- **Tree edge.** Head $v$ is undiscovered ($d[v]$ unassigned), making $u$ the parent of $v$.
- **Back edge.** Head $v$ is active on the stack ($d[v] < d[u] < f[u] < f[v]$), pointing to an ancestor.
- **Forward edge.** Head $v$ is finished and was discovered after $u$ ($d[u] < d[v] < f[v] < f[u]$), skipping down to a descendant.
- **Cross edge.** Head $v$ finished before $u$ was discovered ($d[v] < f[v] < d[u] < f[u]$), bridging between separate branches.

The back edge alone governs cycles:

$$
G \text{ has a cycle} \iff \text{any depth-first search of } G \text{ produces a back edge}
$$

If arc $(u, v)$ is a back edge, the tree path from ancestor $v$ to $u$ combined with $(u, v)$ forms a closed cycle. Conversely, on any cycle, the earliest entered vertex $v$ must eventually receive a back edge from its cyclic predecessor.

In a directed acyclic graph (DAG), sorting vertices in decreasing order of finish time $f[v]$ produces a valid topological order. Because a DAG has no back edges, every arc $(u, v)$ satisfies $f[u] > f[v]$.

## Anatomy of Three Critical Frames

Scrub the player to three revealing frames:

- **Frame 9.** The stack holds $[A, B, D, E]$ when arc $E \to A$ is inspected. Vertex $A$ sits at the stack bottom, meaning this single frame captures the cycle $A \to B \to D \to E \to A$ as a back edge.
- **Frame 26.** Arc $A \to E$ is examined after $E$ has already finished at time 5. This is a forward edge: an optimization shortcut rather than a cycle.
- **Frame 18.** Arc $C \to D$ reaches $D$ after $D$'s entire subtree has closed. Because $D$ is neither ancestor nor descendant of $C$, this is a cross edge.

## Topological Ordering, Cut-Vertices, and Strong Components

Run the search on a DAG and the finishing order delivers a topological sort without additional bookkeeping:

Reversing the exit times $E, D, B, C, A$ yields the topological order $A, C, B, D, E$. Every directed edge points strictly forward in this sequence, generated without in-degree counting or queueing.

Define $low[w]$ as the minimum discovery time reachable from $w$'s subtree via at most one non-tree arc. This single metric enables Tarjan's linear-time algorithms for cut-vertices and biconnectivity (Hopcroft and Tarjan 1973).

The same timestamps identify Strongly Connected Components (SCCs) in a single $O(V + E)$ pass (Tarjan 1972). Modern optimizing compilers build dominator trees on control-flow graphs using this identical interval geometry (Lengauer and Tarjan 1979).

## Attributions

- **Tarjan 1972** — *Depth-first search and linear graph algorithms*, SIAM Journal on Computing: the linear-time strong-components algorithm, and the paper that made the forest plus two time-stamps the substrate of graph algorithms.
- **Hopcroft and Tarjan 1973** — *Efficient algorithms for graph manipulation*, CACM: biconnected components and articulation points from the same discovery times, via the low-link values used above.
- **Knuth 1968** — *The Art of Computer Programming*, Vol. 1: the systematic treatment of backtracking, of which depth-first search is the case on a graph.
- **Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein** — *Introduction to Algorithms*: the parenthesis theorem, the white-path theorem and the four-way classification of arcs, stated here as they state them.
- **Lengauer and Tarjan 1979** — dominator trees from a depth-first traversal of a control-flow graph.
- **Trémaux 1882** — the maze-walking method, recorded in Lucas's *Récréations Mathématiques*, the earliest written depth-first traversal.
