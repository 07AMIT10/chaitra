## The Expanding Horizon

In 1956, sitting on a café terrace in Amsterdam, Edsger Dijkstra conceived an algorithm in twenty minutes without pen or paper. He did not search for paths; he simulated a wave of light expanding outward through glass of varying densities. The moment that wave touches a town, its shortest distance is carved into stone forever.

## The Fallacy of Destination

A standard textbook frames this as finding the shortest path between a source `s` and a destination `t`. That framing obscures what is really computed. The execution never hunts for a target: it stops only when the frontier empties, having priced every reachable town in the network.

The run returns a distance tree rather than a single trajectory. A path to `t` exists only because each settled town remembers the road it arrived on. Erase those back-pointers and the algorithm still answers the true question it was asked: what is the metric distance to every point in space?

"Search" is the wrong metaphor. A search terminates upon discovering its goal; Dijkstra terminates only when the entire reachable universe has been measured.

## The Monotone Ceiling

Fix a weighted digraph with weights $w(u,v) \ge 0$, and a source $A$. Write $\delta(v)$ for the least cost of any walk from $A$ to $v$.

The state consists of two mathematical structures: a set $S$ of **settled** vertices, and a label $d(v)$ per vertex held to a strict invariant:

$$
d(v) = \min\{\, \text{cost}(A \to v) : \text{every interior vertex of the walk lies in } S \,\}
$$

Read the invariant plainly: $d(v)$ is the cheapest route to $v$ restricted to paths that only step through settled towns. At the start $S = \{A\}$, so each label reflects either a direct road or $\infty$.

Two operations maintain this invariant. **Settle** the unsettled vertex $u$ with the minimum label, adding it to $S$. **Relax** each outgoing road $(u, v)$ by setting $d(v) \leftarrow \min\bigl(d(v), d(u) + w(u,v)\bigr)$.

Think of each label as an upper bound. Every label represents a known valid walk, ensuring $d(v) \ge \delta(v)$ at all times. Relaxation replaces $d(v)$ with a smaller walk cost, driving labels monotonically downward toward the truth without ever overshooting.

## The Greedy Exchange and the Negative Abyss

Let $u$ be the unsettled vertex with the smallest tentative label. The algorithm freezes $u$ into the settled set and declares $d(u)$ final. That claim is Dijkstra's 1959 greedy exchange argument.

Assume by induction that all previously settled labels in $S$ are correct. Consider any competing walk from $A$ to $u$ that leaves $S$, and let $(x, y)$ be its first road with $x \in S$ and $y \notin S$.

The walk splits cleanly at $y$. The prefix to $y$ costs at least $d(x) + w(x,y) \ge d(y)$ because relaxing $x$ already lowered $d(y)$. If all road weights are non-negative, the remainder of the walk from $y$ to $u$ cannot have negative cost:

$$
\text{cost}(A \to u) = \underbrace{\text{cost}(A \to y)}_{\ge\, d(y)} + \underbrace{\text{cost}(y \to u)}_{\ge\, 0} \ge d(y) \ge d(u)
$$

No alternate route can undercut $d(u)$, so $d(u) = \delta(u)$ is permanent the instant it is settled. The exchange holds because the cheapest route out of the settled horizon is already globally optimal.

Notice where the non-negativity hypothesis lives. The term $\text{cost}(y \to u) \ge 0$ holds only because no road subtracts time. Introduce a negative road, and future travel can refund cost, collapsing the inequality.

Algebra gives this dynamic its proper name. Define $\oplus = \min$ and $\otimes = +$, with identity $\infty$ for $\oplus$ and identity $0$ for $\otimes$. This is the **min-plus semiring**, the tropical algebra of optimization.

Construct the weight matrix $W$ where $W_{uv} = w(u,v)$, diagonal entries are $0$, and absent roads are $\infty$. The settled distance vector is simply a single row of the matrix closure:

$$
d(v) = (W^{*})_{Av}, \qquad W^{*} = \bigoplus_{k \ge 0} W^{\otimes k}
$$

The closure $W^*$ represents all-pairs shortest paths. Floyd (1962) and Warshall (1962) compute every row with a triple loop, and Lehmann (1977) proved this loop solves metric closures over any semiring. Dijkstra computes a single row of this tropical universe.

## Priority Queues and Continental Routing

Every settlement step performs an extract-min operation. Scanning an array for the minimum label costs $O(n)$ per step and $O(n^2)$ total. A binary heap reduces the cost to $O((m+n)\log n)$ on $m$ edges, while a Fibonacci heap achieves $O(m + n\log n)$ by making relaxation constant time (Fredman and Tarjan 1984).

Two conclusions emerge from the data. The array scan tracks $n^2/2$ with precision, reflecting the sum of shrinking linear searches. The binary heap tracks $n \log n$, requiring 285 times fewer comparisons on a network of 12,800 vertices.

Evaluating the same recurrence over other semirings produces different classic algorithms. Replacing $\oplus$ with $\max$ yields Viterbi decoding for hidden Markov models. Substituting edit costs yields sequence alignment, while Boolean $(\text{OR}, \text{AND})$ computes transitive closure.

A* accelerates point-to-point queries by adding a goal-directed potential to the priority key (Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael 1968). Modern GPS navigation scales this further with contraction hierarchies, answering queries across continental road networks in microseconds (Geisberger et al. 2008).

## When Light Loops: The Absence of Negative Cycles

The min-plus closure converges to finite values if and only if no directed cycle has negative total weight. Trapped in a negative cycle, a walk accumulates negative infinity as it loops.

Bellman (1958) embraced a different philosophy. By relaxing every edge $n-1$ times without freezing a settled set, Bellman–Ford tolerates negative edges in $O(nm)$ time.

The settled set is the critical boundary. Non-negative edges are the sole condition that makes greed provably exact.
