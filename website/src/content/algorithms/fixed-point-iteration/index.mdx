## The Cobweb Spiral
<ChapterMark minutes={3} tag="the hook" />

At contraction factor $q = 0.9999$, one correct digit costs 23,025 steps. At $q = 0.6736$ it costs 5.83. Type `cos` into a pocket calculator, press equals twice, and no matter what you entered the display drifts to `0.739085...`, the Dottie number.

The picture is **the cobweb**: bounce between $y = g(x)$ and $y = x$; a contraction shortens every bounce by $q$, and a point with $|g'| > 1$ repels.

## The Mirage of the Halting Loop
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the trap" />

You want to solve $x = g(x)$, so you iterate $x_{n+1} = g(x_n)$ and stop when the points stop moving. For $g(x) = \cos x$ this works from any start you drag the slider to.

The operational framing hides the real question. Some maps land and some run away, and the loop itself cannot tell you which kind you hold. The question is what property of $g$ makes iteration a proof instead of a hope.

## Complete Spaces and Metric Contractions
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the invariant" />

The real object is a complete metric space with a contraction on it. A metric space is a set with a distance $d(x, y)$, and completeness means every Cauchy sequence has a limit in the space. The closed interval of the real line is complete; an open interval is not, since a sequence can converge to a missing endpoint.

A contraction is a map $g$ with one number $q$ below $1$ such that $d(g(x), g(y)) \le q\,d(x, y)$ for every pair $x$ and $y$. The constant $q$ is the whole story: it says all distances shrink by the same factor at every step. The panel's ratio column is your window onto $q$.

## Banach's Inevitable Attractor
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="the algebra" />

Banach's contraction mapping theorem (Banach 1922, building on Picard's 1890 successive approximations) states the payoff. If $g$ is a contraction with constant $q$ on a complete metric space, then $g$ has exactly one fixed point $x^*$, and $x_{n+1} = g(x_n)$ converges to it from any start.

The proof is short, and each hypothesis is used exactly once. First, successive distances shrink geometrically: $d(x_n, x_{n+1}) \le q^n\,d(x_0, x_1)$, by applying the contraction $n$ times. This is where $q$ below $1$ does its work.

Next, the iterates form a Cauchy sequence, by the triangle inequality and the geometric sum:

$$
d(x_n, x_m) \le \frac{q^n}{1 - q}\,d(x_0, x_1), \qquad m > n.
$$

Completeness is used here and only here: it promotes this Cauchy sequence to a limit $x^*$ in the space. Continuity of $g$ then gives $g(x^*) = x^*$.

Uniqueness needs no completeness at all. Two fixed points would satisfy $d(x^*, y^*) \le q\,d(x^*, y^*)$, which forces the distance to zero since $q$ is below $1$.

Letting $m$ grow without bound in the Cauchy estimate gives the a priori bound (Banach 1922):

$$
d(x_n, x^*) \le \frac{q^n}{1 - q}\,d(x_0, x_1).
$$

It predicts the error before you iterate, from the first step alone. A one-step version gives the a posteriori bound, which prices the error you have already earned:

$$
d(x_{n+1}, x^*) \le \frac{q}{1 - q}\,d(x_n, x_{n+1}).
$$

To derive it, apply the contraction to $d(x_{n+1}, x^*) = d(g(x_n), g(x^*))$ to get $d(x_{n+1}, x^*) \le q\,d(x_n, x^*)$, then split $d(x_n, x^*)$ through $x_{n+1}$ by the triangle inequality and rearrange. Both bounds carry the same factor $1/(1-q)$: the price of summing the whole tail of the staircase.

The check you actually run is calculus. If $|g'(x)| \le q$ below $1$ on a closed interval, the mean value theorem (Lagrange 1797) gives $|g(x) - g(y)| = |g'(\xi)|\,|x - y| \le q\,|x - y|$. So $g$ is a contraction there, and the theorem applies.

For $g(x) = \cos x$ the derivative is $-\sin x$, and at the fixed point $x^* = 0.7390851332151$ its size is $|\sin x^*| = 0.6736$. That is the number the panel's ratio column settles on, and the curve bends toward it one factor of $0.6736$ per step.

The cobweb is this proof drawn as geometry. Going vertical from $(x_n, x_n)$ to the curve reaches $(x_n, g(x_n))$, and going horizontal to the diagonal carries that height to the next point $(x_{n+1}, x_{n+1})$.

When $q$ reaches $1$ the guarantee dies, and the same loop can misbehave completely. The logistic map $g(x) = r\,x(1 - x)$ is fixed-point iteration with a different $g$, and as $r$ grows it doubles its period until the iterates turn chaotic (May 1976). Contraction is the boundary between this page and that chaos.

Newton's method is the special case where the derivative vanishes at the answer. Its map $N(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)$ satisfies $N'(x^*) = 0$ at a simple root, so the linear factor $q$ collapses and the error squares instead of shrinking geometrically. That is why Newton converges quadratically while this page converges at rate $q^n$; the section's [Newton page](/algorithms/programming/newton-method) proves the squaring.

Name the contrast that makes Banach 1922 worth its fame. Brouwer's fixed-point theorem (Brouwer 1911) says a continuous map of a closed ball to itself has a fixed point: existence with no uniqueness, no rate, and no way to compute it. Banach trades the weak hypothesis for a strong one and returns existence, uniqueness, and a computable error bound in one stroke.

## From Picard Iteration to PageRank
<ChapterMark minutes={2} tag="systems" />

Picard used this iteration to solve differential equations (Picard 1890): rewrite $y' = f(y)$ as an integral equation and iterate the integral, which contracts on a short time interval. Banach's abstraction turned one existence proof into a method for equations everywhere.

Every solver with a "tolerance" and a "max iterations" field is spending this theorem. Value iteration in dynamic programming, power iteration for eigenvectors, and the inner loops of splitting methods all converge because some map contracts. When they stall, the first diagnostic is the same: estimate $q$, and check whether it is really below $1$.

## Escaping the Basin: When Contraction is Local

Skippable. The theorem needs $g$ to map a complete set into itself and contract there. Often neither holds globally, yet both hold on a small closed ball around the fixed point.

Then the same proof runs locally: start inside the ball and you converge with the same rate. Newton lives here, since $N'(x^*) = 0$ only forces contraction near $x^*$. The global cosine map of this page is the easy case, not the typical one.

## Lineage & Attributions

- **Picard 1890** — successive approximations for ordinary differential equations, the iteration Banach later abstracted.
- **Brouwer 1911** — the topological fixed-point theorem: existence without uniqueness, rate, or construction; the foil to Banach.
- **Banach 1922** — the contraction mapping theorem with the a priori and a posteriori error bounds; the spine of this page.
- **Lagrange 1797** — the mean value theorem, which turns the derivative check $|g'| \le q$ into a contraction.
- **May 1976** — period doubling and chaos in the logistic map, showing what the same iteration does once $q \ge 1$.
