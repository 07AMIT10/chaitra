## Floyd–Warshall is not an all-pairs shortest-path algorithm. It closes one matrix over a semiring — and the arithmetic you choose is the problem you are solving.

Four towns, five one-way roads. Order the towns A, B, C, D and every road runs downhill, so no trip can loop forever.

```
A --1--> B --1--> D
 \        |        ^
  1       1        1
   \      v        |
    ---> C --------+
```

The roads are one grid of numbers, `R`, where `R[i][j]` is the cost of driving straight from `i` to `j` and a dot means there is no road.

```
      A  B  C  D
 A    ·  1  1  ·
 B    ·  ·  1  1
 C    ·  ·  ·  1
 D    ·  ·  ·  ·
```

That grid is the entire input. Fix your attention on one cell, A→D, and ask what number belongs in it.

## 1 · The question

Here is the whole algorithm.

```
for k = 1 … n
  for i = 1 … n
    for j = 1 … n
      R[i][j] ← R[i][j] ⊕ R[i][k] ⊗ R[k][j]
```

Read what is missing. No town, no road, no distance, no queue, no priority. Nowhere does the code say what ⊕ and ⊗ are.

The operational framing — "find all shortest paths" — smuggles that answer in, quietly setting ⊕ to min and ⊗ to plus. That is a choice. It is the only choice in the program, and every meaning the loop can carry lives inside it.

The naive alternatives are also worse. Dijkstra from every source costs $O(n\,m\log n)$ and refuses negative edges. Bellman–Ford from every source costs $O(n^2 m)$. The four lines above cost $O(n^3)$, allow negative edges, and never ask what the numbers mean.

## 2 · The real object: a matrix over a semiring

A **semiring** is a set $S$ with two operations, $\oplus$ and $\otimes$, and two elements, $0$ and $1$, obeying four rules (Golan 1999):

- $(S,\oplus,0)$ is a commutative monoid — $\oplus$ is associative and commutative with identity $0$.
- $(S,\otimes,1)$ is a monoid — $\otimes$ is associative with identity $1$.
- $\otimes$ distributes over $\oplus$ on both sides: $a\otimes(b\oplus c) = (a\otimes b)\oplus(a\otimes c)$, and the mirror image.
- $0$ annihilates: $a\otimes 0 = 0\otimes a = 0$.

That is the whole list, and the loop of §1 uses nothing else. Associativity lets you regroup a chain of roads. Distributivity lets you split a route at a point. Annihilation lets a route that does not exist add nothing.

A **closed semiring** adds a star: every $a$ has $a^\star$ satisfying

$$ a^\star = 1 \oplus a \otimes a^\star = 1 \oplus a^\star \otimes a $$

and when the series converges, $a^\star = 1 \oplus a \oplus a^2 \oplus a^3 \oplus \cdots$. The star is the closure. Kleene 1951 introduced it for regular languages; for a matrix $A$ the loop computes $A^\star$, the least solution of $X = I \oplus A \otimes X$ (Lehmann 1977). Four instances carry this page:

| semiring | ⊕ | ⊗ | zero | one | the entry $A^\star_{ij}$ means |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(\min,+)$ | $\min$ | $+$ | $+\infty$ | $0$ | cheapest route |
| $(+,\times)$ | $+$ | $\times$ | $0$ | $1$ | number of routes |
| $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ | OR | AND | false | true | whether a route exists |
| $(\max,+)$ | $\max$ | $+$ | $-\infty$ | $0$ | longest route |

Each row is a different arithmetic obeying the same four rules, and the four lines cannot tell the rows apart.

## 3 · The mathematics

**The recurrence.** Let $d^{(k)}_{ij}$ be the entry after the first $k$ rounds. Warshall 1962 gives the Boolean version and Floyd 1962 the weighted one; both are this:

$$ d^{(0)}_{ij} = A_{ij} \oplus \delta_{ij}, \qquad d^{(k)}_{ij} = d^{(k-1)}_{ij} \oplus \bigl( d^{(k-1)}_{ik} \otimes d^{(k-1)}_{kj} \bigr) $$

The $\delta_{ij}$ term is $1$ when $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. It is the road of length zero from a town to itself, and it is why the closure includes staying put. After $n$ rounds $d^{(n)} = A^\star$.

**Why the axioms are enough.** A route from $i$ to $j$ either avoids $k$ or passes through $k$. Distributivity turns "or" into $\oplus$ and "then" into $\otimes$; associativity lets the split sit anywhere in the route; the zero absorbs the routes that do not exist. Nothing outside the four rules is used, which is why the same four lines answer four unrelated questions.

**The step is an elimination with a star.** Round $k$ removes town $k$ from every remaining route. A route that enters $k$ may lap through $k$ any number of times before it leaves, and that freedom is not a finite sum — it is the star $R_{kk}^{\star} = \delta \oplus R_{kk} \oplus R_{kk}^{2} \oplus \cdots$. So the exact step replaces the pivot by its star, scales row $k$ and column $k$ by it, and only then splits each remaining entry into "avoid $k$" or "enter, lap through $k$, leave".

When $\oplus$ is idempotent — $a \oplus a = a$, as for $\min$, $\max$ and OR — the star collapses to $\delta \oplus R_{kk}$, and seeding $\delta$ on the diagonal up front changes nothing. Addition is the one non-idempotent case: $1 \oplus 1 = 2$, so a lap is a genuinely new route and the star is the geometric series $\frac{1}{1 - R_{kk}}$. The panel seeds the identity only where the collapse holds, and computes the star where it does not.

**Four readings of this grid.** Point at the cell A→D after the closure:

| reading | A→D | the number is |
|---|---|---|
| $(\min,+)$ | 2 | the fewest roads on a route |
| $(\max,+)$ | 3 | the most roads on a route |
| $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ | 1 (true) | whether D is reachable |
| $(+,\times)$ | 3 | how many distinct routes |

Under $(\max,+)$ and under $(+,\times)$ the cell prints 3, and the two threes count different things: three roads on the single longest route, three routes in total. That is a coincidence of this grid, not an identity. The diagonal tells the same story — $(\min,+)$ and $(\max,+)$ put 0 there, $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ puts true, and $(+,\times)$ puts 1, the empty route. Every panel agrees with its own row, cell for cell.

**The star as a matrix inverse.** Over the reals under $(+,\times)$, with no cycle, the closure is the geometric series

$$ A^\star = I + A + A^2 + A^3 + \cdots = (I - A)^{-1} $$

so the triple loop is Gaussian elimination computing a matrix inverse in a semiring (Lehmann 1977). Cheapest routes, route counts, reachability and longest routes are all one inverse, taken in four arithmetics.

## 5 · The payoff

**Shortest paths.** $(\min,+)$ is Floyd 1962's reading, and the one that answers all-pairs distances in a single cubic pass. Negative roads are allowed; if a diagonal entry is negative after round $n$, some route is cheaper than standing still, and that is a negative cycle.

**Reachability.** $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ is Warshall 1962's transitive closure. Databases use it to answer "is there a path from x to y" without walking the edges; build tools use it to close a dependency relation and order the work.

**Maximum-likelihood paths.** Viterbi decoding of a hidden Markov model is $(\max,\times)$; take logs and it is $(\max,+)$. The trellis and this grid carry the same recurrence, which is why speech recognisers, gene finders and parsers all contain this loop.

**Routing.** Distance-vector routing is the $(\min,+)$ closure spread across routers, each holding one row. A router relaxes its row against a neighbour's, and a link failure is just a changed entry.

**Capacity.** The sibling arithmetic $(\max,\min)$ maximises the smallest capacity along a route. That is the widest-path question, the natural one for a network asked for bandwidth rather than distance.

**Counting.** $(+,\times)$ counts routes. On a grid with cycles it counts walks, which is cheap; counting *simple* paths instead is #P-complete (Valiant 1979). The arithmetic you pick decides which side of that cliff you stand on.

## 6 · Advanced, and skippable

Skip this on a first read. It is the fine print of existence.

**When the star exists.** The closure sums over routes of unbounded length, so the grid decides whether the sum converges.

| reading | the closure exists exactly when |
|---|---|
| $(\min,+)$ | no cycle has negative total cost; otherwise the sum runs down forever |
| $(\max,+)$ | no cycle has positive total cost; otherwise the sum runs up forever |
| $(+,\times)$ | the grid has no cycle — otherwise there are infinitely many walks (weights below 1 are also fine) |
| $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ | always; a Boolean cycle adds no new truth |

$(\max,+)$ is unbounded above on any positive cycle, and that is the only reason it needs an acyclic grid. $(\min,+)$ has the mirror problem below.

**The star is where the four readings part company.** Every panel runs the same elimination; they differ only in the number the pivot is replaced by. For $(\min,+)$ and $(\max,+)$ a diagonal entry is $\delta$ unless a cycle through that town pays off — negative for $\min$, positive for $\max$ — so the star is $\delta$ and every lap is free. For $(\mathrm{OR},\mathrm{AND})$ a Boolean lap adds no truth, so the star is true. For $(+,\times)$ the star is the geometric series, and that is the whole difference: it equals the plain sum exactly while $R_{kk} = 0$, and diverges the moment a lap exists, which is precisely the condition that no cycle passes through $k$.

On this grid every diagonal entry stays 0, so the $(+,\times)$ panel settles at the true count. A→D reads 3 — two routes of two roads and one of three — and it stops there, because there is no lap to add.

The true count is the geometric series, and the reason it settles here is that the grid has no cycle. Your cell A→D collects two routes of two roads and one of three, then stops. Put the towns on a cycle instead and every extra lap is another route, so nothing ever stops.

**Cost.** The loop is $O(n^3)$ in time and $O(n^2)$ in space if you keep the whole grid. On a sparse graph with non-negative weights, $n$ runs of Dijkstra beat it — $O(n\,m\log n)$ against $O(n^3)$. On a dense graph, or one with negative edges, the four lines win.
