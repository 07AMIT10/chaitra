## Longest common subsequence is not the letters the two strings share. It is the tallest chain in the product of their prefix orders.

Read the two strings as rows, and cross out characters until the survivors agree.

```
A B C B D A B
B D C A B A
```

The word `BCAB` survives in each: the top row keeps positions 2, 3, 4, 6, the bottom keeps 1, 3, 4, 5. Try to keep five characters and you stall at four.

Four is a **length**, not a word. That gap runs the whole page: measuring the length is cheap, printing one word is cheap, and counting all the words is not.

## 1 · The question

Operationally you are handed two strings, $a$ of length $m$ and $b$ of length $n$, and asked for the longest word that is a **subsequence** — a selection of positions in order, not necessarily adjacent — of both. Brute force lists all $2^m$ subsequences of $a$ and keeps the longest that also sits in $b$. That is exponential work for an answer that is one integer.

The working fix is dynamic. Let $L(i,j)$ be the length of the longest common subsequence of the prefixes $a[1..i]$ and $b[1..j]$:

$$
L(i,j) \;=\; \begin{cases} 0, & i = 0 \text{ or } j = 0,\\[2pt] L(i-1,j-1) + 1, & a_i = b_j,\\[2pt] \max\bigl(L(i-1,j),\; L(i,j-1)\bigr), & a_i \ne b_j. \end{cases}
$$

That recurrence is correct, and it never mentions a subsequence. It compares prefixes and takes maxima. What it measures is a *height* in an order built from the two strings, and that order is the object we actually want.

## 2 · The real object: a grid, and the chains of matching cells

Take the prefixes of $a$ in order: $\varepsilon \subset a[1..1] \subset \dots \subset a[1..m]$. Prefixes order by extension, so this is a chain — a totally ordered set — with $m+1$ elements. The prefixes of $b$ form a second chain with $n+1$ elements.

The **product** of the two chains is the grid of prefix pairs. Its element $(i,j)$ is the pair $(a[1..i],\, b[1..j])$, and $(i,j) \preceq (i',j')$ exactly when $i \le i'$ **and** $j \le j'$. A product of two chains is a lattice, and this one — the $(m+1)\times(n+1)$ grid — is the **prefix lattice** of the pair of strings.

Inside it sit the **matching cells**

$$
M \;=\; \{\, (i,j) \;:\; a_i = b_j \,\},
$$

carrying the same product order. Now look at what a chain of $M$ is. A chain is a set of matching cells, pairwise comparable, so its rows strictly increase and its columns strictly increase. Its cells pair $a_{i_1},\dots,a_{i_t}$ with $b_{j_1},\dots,b_{j_t}$, and every pair agrees, so both sequences spell one word — a common subsequence of length $t$. Every common subsequence arises this way, so

$$
\mathrm{LCS}(a,b) \;=\; \operatorname{height}(M),
$$

the longest chain among the matching cells.

The DP table is this lattice with a label on every cell: $L(i,j)$ is the height of $M$ inside the rectangle $[1..i]\times[1..j]$ — the tallest chain reachable using only the first $i$ characters of $a$ and the first $j$ of $b$. Hover a cell in section 4 and that height is the number you are reading.

## 3 · The mathematics

### 3.1 The recurrence, from the last matched pair

Fix a longest chain in the rectangle $[1..i]\times[1..j]$ and inspect its last cell, the one with the largest row and column. Exactly one of three things holds.

- The last cell is $(i,j)$, which needs $a_i = b_j$. Everything before it lies in $[1..i-1]\times[1..j-1]$, so the chain has length $L(i-1,j-1)+1$.
- The last cell is not in row $i$, so the chain already lives in $[1..i-1]\times[1..j]$ and has length $L(i-1,j)$.
- The last cell is not in column $j$, giving $L(i,j-1)$.

When $a_i = b_j$ the first case beats the other two, and when $a_i \ne b_j$ the cell $(i,j)$ is not in $M$ so only the last two remain. Those three cases are the recurrence above. The $O(mn)$ table is Wagner and Fischer 1974, written for string-to-string correction; the common subsequence is the case where substitution is forbidden.

### 3.2 A chain is a staircase, and the table is its longest path

Draw the grid with the row index down and the column index across. A chain in $M$ is a set of cells you visit in order, never moving left or up. Thread a path through them: between consecutive cells step **down** to raise the row or **right** to raise the column. Join the first cell to $(0,0)$ and the last to $(m,n)$ the same way.

Every path from $(0,0)$ to $(m,n)$ built from down-steps, right-steps and diagonal steps is an **alignment** of the two strings.

- A down-step consumes one character of $a$ and none of $b$ — a deletion.
- A right-step consumes one character of $b$ and none of $a$ — an insertion.
- A diagonal step consumes one of each, and when it lands on a matching cell it is a matched pair.

Restrict the diagonal steps to matching cells and the correspondence is exact. Each such staircase picks out the diagonal cells $(i_1,j_1),\dots,(i_t,j_t)$, both coordinates increasing, each pair equal — a chain of $M$ and nothing more. The staircase is the chain, and the chain is the common subsequence. Different staircases can spell the same word: `aab` and `aba` share `ab` along two different staircases. That gap between *witnesses* and *words* returns in section 3.4.

Read the table as a longest path and $L(i,j)$ is the greatest number of match-diagonal steps on any staircase from $(0,0)$ to $(i,j)$. The three cases of the recurrence are the three possible last moves, and the arrows that light up when you hover a cell are exactly those moves.

### 3.3 Insert and delete, and the distance identity

Price a down-step and a right-step at one each, and a matching diagonal step at zero. A staircase from $(0,0)$ to $(m,n)$ with $t$ match-diagonal steps spends $m-t$ down-steps deleting the characters of $a$ it skips and $n-t$ right-steps inserting the characters of $b$ it adds, so its cost is

$$
(m-t) + (n-t) \;=\; m + n - 2t .
$$

The cheapest staircase maximises $t$, and the largest $t$ is $\mathrm{LCS}(a,b)$. Therefore the insert/delete edit distance is

$$
d_{\text{ins/del}}(a,b) \;=\; m + n - 2\,\mathrm{LCS}(a,b),
$$

a derived identity, not a definition: it is the staircase cost above at $t = t^{*}$. It is the same identity the edit distance page reaches from the other side, and it is the honest answer to the next prompt.

### 3.4 Length, witness, count

The height is one number, and the table computes it in $O(mn)$ time and space. Press **Trace solution** and the highlighted chain walks one witness out — a staircase of $L(m,n)$ matched cells, one longest common subsequence you can print. Keeping the whole table to trace it costs $O(mn)$ memory; Hirschberg 1975 reconstructs the same witness in $O(\min(m,n))$ space by splitting the strings in half, computing the table from both ends, and recursing on the winning half.

Counting the *distinct* longest common subsequences is a different order of problem. Take $2k$ distinct symbols $x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k$ and set

```
a = x₁ y₁ x₂ y₂ ⋯ x_k y_k
b = y₁ x₁ y₂ x₂ ⋯ y_k x_k
```

In $a$ the two symbols of each block appear one way round; in $b$ the other way. A common subsequence can use at most one symbol from any block — using both would need the two orders to agree — and it needs one from every block to reach the maximum. The maximum length is therefore $k$, and every choice of one symbol per block is a distinct longest common subsequence. That is $2^k$ words on strings of length $n = 2k$: the count is $2^{\,n/2}$, exponential in the length.

One caution if you try to count. A DP that sums its predecessor counts counts *staircases*, not *words*, and the two differ: `aab` and `aba` reach the word `ab` by two different staircases, so a predecessor-summing counter overcounts. Counting witnesses is easy; counting words is the hard half, and it is hard because the answer itself can be exponential while the input is linear.

The top row and left column are all zero, because an empty prefix shares no character with anything. An interior cell on a match copies its upper-left neighbour and adds one — a diagonal step of the staircase. Off a match it takes the larger of the neighbours above and to the left, a down-step or a right-step. The bottom-right cell reads 4.

Turn on **Trace solution** and the chain walks out one witness: four matched cells joined into a staircase, spelling `BCAB`. Different tie-breaks inside the table produce different witnesses of the same length — that ambiguity is the seed of section 3.4, where the witnesses can outnumber the words.

## 5 · The payoff

- **`diff`, `patch`, and version control.** A line diff is a longest common subsequence over lines. Myers 1986 turned the staircase into an $O(nd)$ walk along its diagonals, where $d$ is the edit distance, so `diff`, `patch` and `git diff` cost the size of the change rather than the size of the files. The picture a diff tool draws is the staircase of section 3.2.
- **Sequence alignment.** Needleman and Wunsch 1970 scored this recurrence over amino acids, and Smith and Waterman 1981 dropped the requirement to span both strings. Flip a mismatch to a negative score and the same grid aligns locally. Every alignment score in bioinformatics is this table.
- **Longest increasing subsequence.** When $b$ is a rearrangement of $a$ with all characters distinct, send each character of $b$ to its position in $a$ to get a permutation $\pi$. Then $\mathrm{LCS}(a,b) = \mathrm{LIS}(\pi)$, because a common subsequence is a set of positions increasing in both strings. For `1234` and `2143`, $\pi = 2,1,4,3$ and its longest increasing subsequence has length 2 — the same 2 the table gives. That is the bridge to the [Longest Increasing Subsequence](/algorithms/programming/longest-increasing-subsequence) page, where this number is the height of a two-dimensional order.
- **Plagiarism and duplicate detection.** Comparing documents, source files, or product titles is an LCS or an edit distance over tokens, and the same staircase decides which spans match.

## 6 · Advanced (optional): faster tables and the quadratic wall

The exact $O(mn)$ table is not the last word when the strings share few characters. Hunt and Szymanski 1977 listed only the matching cells and ran an LIS-like pass over them, for $O((r+n)\log n)$ with $r = |M|$ — a win when $r$ is small. Masek and Paterson 1980 used the four-Russians method to reach $O(n^2/\log n)$. Crochemore, Iliopoulos, Pinzon and Reid 2001 packed the DP into machine words for $O(nm/w)$ bit operations.

Against all of that, the quadratic shape is close to unavoidable. Abboud, Backurs and Williams 2015 show that an $O(n^{2-\epsilon})$ algorithm for LCS would refute the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis. State it at its true strength: a conditional lower bound, not a proof that no fast algorithm exists. It is why the grid has stood since Wagner and Fischer 1974.
