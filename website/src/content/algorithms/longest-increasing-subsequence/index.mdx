## Longest increasing subsequence is not a hunt for a subsequence. It is the tallest chain in a two-dimensional order.

Six numbers stand in a row: `4 1 5 2 6 3`. You may keep any of them, in the order they appear, but the kept numbers have to climb the whole way. Lock a guess at the longest such run.

## 1 · The question

The ask is operational. Walk left to right, decide for each number whether to keep it, and finish with the longest increasing thing you kept. A subsequence is any subset of positions, so brute force has $2^n$ candidates to inspect.

The usual fix is dynamic. Let `dp[i]` be the length of the longest increasing subsequence that ends exactly at position `i`.

$$
dp[i] \;=\; 1 + \max\{\, dp[j] \;:\; j < i \text{ and } p_j < p_i \,\}, \qquad dp[i] = 1 \text{ when no such } j \text{ exists.}
$$

That recurrence is correct, and it is not the question. After the base case it never mentions a subsequence; it only compares a number to earlier numbers that happen to be smaller. What it maximises is a *chain* in an order built on the points, and that order is the object we actually want.

## 2 · The real object: the points, in two orders at once

Keep the sequence, drop the list, and draw the points $(i, p_i)$ in the plane. Write $(i, p_i) \prec (j, p_j)$ when $i < j$ **and** $p_i < p_j$. Both coordinates must move the same way: the position grows and the value grows.

That relation is a strict partial order. Nothing precedes itself, and if $a \prec b$ and $b \prec c$ then $a \prec c$. It is the intersection of the natural order on positions with the natural order on values, which is why it is called a two-dimensional order, or the dominance order on the points.

A **chain** is a set whose points are pairwise comparable. Listed by position, its values strictly increase, which is exactly an increasing subsequence and nothing more. An **antichain** is a set with no comparable pair: for $i < j$ we need $p_i \ge p_j$, so listed by position its values never rise.

The **height** of the order is the length of its longest chain; the **width** is the size of its largest antichain. In the panel above, gold is a longest chain and blue is a largest antichain. Every quantity the original problem asks for is a coordinate of this one picture.

## 3 · The mathematics: one cover for the height, one cover for the width

**Mirsky's theorem (Mirsky 1971).** The height of a finite poset equals the fewest antichains that cover it.

$$
\operatorname{height}(P) \;=\; \min\{\, k \;:\; P = A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_k, \ \text{each } A_i \text{ an antichain} \,\}
$$

Patience sorting is a proof you can watch. Deal the numbers face up, left to right, each onto the leftmost pile whose top is at least it; if no top is that large, start a new pile.

Each pile is non-increasing in position order, so each pile is an antichain, and every card lies on exactly one pile. That is a cover of all the points by as many antichains as there are piles, so the height cannot exceed the pile count.

The other direction makes it exact. When a card lands on pile $k$, link it to the top of pile $k-1$ at that moment; following those links from a pile top rebuilds a strictly increasing subsequence of length equal to the number of piles. So the pile count is at least the height, and the two agree.

Together those two facts are Mirsky's theorem made constructive: patience sorting hands you a cover that is not merely valid but *minimum*. The panel prints it as the identity `max chain = min antichain cover`.

What makes it fast is a small invariant. A card lands on the leftmost pile whose top is at least it, so it lowers a top and can never cross the pile to its left; a new pile is started only when the card beats every current top.

Because the tops form a sorted array, each card costs one binary search. With `n` cards and an array of length at most `n`, the pile method runs in $O(n\log n)$ steps against $O(n^2)$ for the recurrence of section 1.

The dual statement is the one routinely swapped with Mirsky's, so keep them apart. Write-ups that call the patience piles "Dilworth" have the attribution backwards.

**Dilworth's theorem (Dilworth 1950).** The width of a finite poset equals the fewest chains that cover it.

$$
\operatorname{width}(P) \;=\; \min\{\, k \;:\; P = C_1 \cup \dots \cup C_k, \ \text{each } C_i \text{ a chain} \,\}
$$

Mirsky covers with antichains and answers the height; Dilworth covers with chains and answers the width. The easy half is a sentence: a chain can hold at most one point of an antichain, so any chain cover needs at least width chains.

That a greedy cover attains the bound is the substantive half, and this page cites it rather than proving it. In the panel both counts are on screen, and they are different numbers.

**Erdős–Szekeres (Erdős and Szekeres 1935).** Every sequence of length greater than $(r-1)(s-1)$ contains a strictly increasing subsequence of length $r$ or a non-increasing subsequence of length $s$.

The proof is a pigeonhole you can run yourself. Give position `i` the pair $(a_i, b_i)$ of the longest increasing and longest non-increasing subsequence ending at `i`. If $i < j$ then either $p_i < p_j$, which forces $a_j > a_i$, or $p_i \ge p_j$, which forces $b_j > b_i$.

So no two positions receive the same pair, and there are only $(r-1)(s-1)$ pairs with $a_i < r$ and $b_i < s$. More positions than pairs forces one of the two runs past its ceiling.

## 4 · The centrepiece: the DP and the piles, on one sequence

Take a sequence where the two sides disagree, so both theorems are visible at once.

Step it and read the columns. The gold chain is `2 → 5 → 7 → 8 → 10 → 13`, of length 6, and the patience piles number 6 as well; that equality is Mirsky's theorem, and the surviving tops `{2, 3, 6, 8, 10, 13}` are the minimum cover the piles built. The blue antichain is `11 → 8 → 6`, of length 3, and the fewest chains that cover the nine points is 3; that is Dilworth's theorem.

Now run the same nine numbers through the other lens. Each cell is `dp[i]`, the longest increasing subsequence ending at that position, filled left to right.

## 5 · The payoff

The height of a two-dimensional order hides inside every problem that stacks or schedules under two thresholds at once.

Box stacking is the same order read off rectangles: box $A$ sits on box $B$ when both sides of $A$ are smaller, and the tallest tower is a longest chain. Partitioning a sequence into the fewest non-increasing runs is the LIS again, by Mirsky, which answers the classic interception problem: how few non-increasing passes are needed to cover an incoming stream.

Two permutations reduce to the same object. Relabel the second by the positions of its values in the first, and the longest common subsequence becomes a longest increasing subsequence; version control leans on precisely this, taking the unique matching lines of two files and keeping a longest increasing subsequence of their positions as diff anchors.

In each case the object is one poset and the certificate is one cover. If a problem hands you a maximum compatible set under two coordinates, you are being asked for a height.

## 6 · Optional: the whole shape, not just its first row

Skippable. If you want the full shape rather than one number, insert each value into a Young tableau by row insertion (Schensted 1961). The shape $\lambda$ that results has first row $\lambda_1$ equal to the longest increasing subsequence, and its first column has length equal to the longest strictly decreasing subsequence.

The patience piles keep only the first row's length; the tableau keeps every row. That extra structure is where the fluctuations of the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation are studied (Baik, Deift and Johansson 1999), a story this page does not enter.

## 7 · Attributions

- **Mirsky 1971** — *A dual of Dilworth's decomposition theorem*: the height of a poset equals the fewest antichains covering it. This is the theorem the patience piles exhibit, and the one the pile count should be credited to.
- **Dilworth 1950** — *A decomposition theorem for partially ordered sets*: the width equals the fewest chains covering it. The dual of Mirsky's statement, not a restatement of it; the page keeps the two apart on purpose.
- **Erdős–Szekeres 1935** — *A combinatorial problem in geometry*: any sequence longer than $(r-1)(s-1)$ contains an increasing subsequence of length $r$ or a non-increasing one of length $s$.
- **Schensted 1961** — *Longest increasing and decreasing subsequences*: insertion into a Young tableau, whose shape has first row the LIS and first column the longest strictly decreasing subsequence.
- **Baik–Deift–Johansson 1999** — the Tracy–Widom limit for the LIS of a random permutation, named only as a pointer from the shape.
