## The tangent line that doubles your digits

## The algebraic recipe versus the geometric truth

Textbooks often introduce root-finding as an imperative loop: compute $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ until $|f(x_n)| < \varepsilon$.

That algebraic formula tells you what to calculate, but obscures what the step actually is. Treating the update as a formula hides both why it converges so furiously and why it can violently diverge.

## The Newton map and its superattracting fixed points

Construct the tangent line to $f(x)$ at the current point $(x_n, f(x_n))$:

$$
y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(x - x_n)
$$

The root of this tangent line defines the next guess. Setting $y = 0$ yields $x = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$, recovering the algebraic rule from basic geometry.

This defines the Newton iteration map $N(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)$. Fixed points where $N(x^*) = x^*$ correspond precisely to roots where $f(x^*) = 0$.

Differentiating the iteration map clarifies its dynamic behavior:

$$
N'(x) = \frac{f(x)\,f''(x)}{f'(x)^2}
$$

At any simple root $f(x^*) = 0$ with $f'(x^*) \neq 0$, the derivative vanishes: $N'(x^*) = 0$. In dynamical systems, a fixed point with zero derivative is **superattracting**: iterates do not merely approach the root, they are sucked in with quadratic velocity (Fatou 1918, Julia 1918).

## Quadratic convergence: why digits double at every step

Let $x^*$ be a simple root and define error $e_n = x_n - x^*$. Expanding $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ in Taylor series around $x^*$:

$$
f(x_n) = f'(x^*)\,e_n + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(x^*)\,e_n^2 + O(e_n^3), \qquad f'(x_n) = f'(x^*) + O(e_n)
$$

Substitute both expansions into the Newton update rule. The first-order linear terms cancel out completely:

$$
e_{n+1} = \frac{f''(x^*)}{2\,f'(x^*)}\,e_n^2 + O(e_n^3)
$$

The subsequent error is proportional to the *square* of the previous error. For $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ at $\sqrt{2}$, the asymptotic error ratio settles on $1 / (2\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.3536$.

This squaring means precision compounds exponentially: 2 correct digits become 4, then 8, 16, 32, and 64.

## Driving the lab: tangents, secants, and the abyss of flat slopes

Switch the interactive lab to Bisection. The geometry shifts from dynamic tangent projections to shrinking brackets, with the error ratio locked at $0.5$. Bisection provides order $p = 1$: safe and guaranteed, but linearly plodding.

Now switch to the Secant method. Instead of computing exact derivatives, it approximates tangents using chords through the last two guesses, delivering golden-ratio order $p = (1+\sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.618$.

Switch to $x^3 - 2x - 5$ and drag the starting point near $0.8165$, where $f'(x) = 0$. The tangent line flattens out, casting the iterate into the mathematical wilderness.

## The engine of numerical computing: from fast inverse square roots to ML

Newton's method forms the bedrock of modern numerical algorithms:

- **Fast reciprocal square root.** Computing $1/\sqrt{x}$ relies on a single Newton step applied to $y^{-2} - x = 0$, operating entirely with multiplications and subtractions without division.
- **Multivariate optimization.** In machine learning and scientific computing, finding extrema of loss functions solves $\nabla f(x) = 0$ using Newton-Raphson, stepping along $H^{-1}\nabla f$ with the Hessian matrix.
- **Hybrid root solvers.** Production libraries run Brent's method (Brent 1973), which combines the guaranteed convergence of bisection with the superlinear speed of secant and inverse quadratic interpolation.

## The fractal frontier: Cayley's problem and Julia sets

In 1879, Arthur Cayley investigated Newton's method on the complex cubic $z^3 - 1 = 0$. He attempted to map which initial starting values converge to which of the three cube roots of unity.

The answer revealed one of the foundational objects of chaos theory. The boundaries separating the three basins of attraction are nowhere smooth: they form an infinitely intricate fractal known as the Julia set.

On the boundaries between basins, Newton iterations bounce chaotically forever. In production numerical software, trust-region protections and line-search backtracking ensure iterations stay anchored within legitimate basins of attraction.

## Historical provenance and attributions

- **Newton 1669** — *De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas*: introduced the geometric tangent root-finding construction on polynomials.
- **Raphson 1690** — *Analysis aequationum universalis*: simplified Newton's approach into the modern algebraic recurrence.
- **Simpson 1740** — Generalized the method to general nonlinear and transcendental equations using fluxions and derivatives.
- **Taylor 1715** — *Methodus Incrementorum Directa et Inversa*: provided the polynomial expansion proving local quadratic convergence $O(e_n^2)$.
- **Fatou 1918 and Julia 1918** — Developed the theory of rational iteration in the complex plane, characterizing superattracting fixed points and fractal basin boundaries.
- **Brent 1973** — *Algorithms for Minimization without Derivatives*: constructed the reliable hybrid root-finding method combining bisection and secant steps.
- **Smale 1986** — *Newton's Method Estimates from Data at One Point*: established point-estimate $\alpha$-theory certifying convergence from single initial values.
