## Stable matching is not a search over matchings. It is deferred acceptance, and the matching that survives the process is the one no pair wants to leave.

The board holds two sides of four names, plus the ranked list each name carries. A join is allowed only when both sides name each other, and the rankings decide how much each name wants it.

## 1 · The question

Suppose a matching is handed to you: each name paired with a partner or left alone. What would make it acceptable? The only test that asks anything of the unmatched ties is whether some left-out pair would rather defect.

Call a pair a **blocking pair** when the left name ranks that right name above its current partner *and* the right name ranks that left name above its own. Such a pair would both prefer to swap, so a matching that contains one is not an equilibrium. A matching with no blocking pair is **stable**.

Checking a finished matching is easy: inspect every left-out pair once. Producing one that passes is the question, and it is not obvious that a stable matching exists at all.

## 2 · The real object: a two-sided market, and the lattice of its outcomes

The input is not a graph. It is two families of preference orders, one order per name, with mutual acceptability meaning that each order lists the other name.

The output is not a single matching either. It is the whole set of matchings that pass the blocking-pair test; on the board that set has exactly two members. So the object to describe is that family, not one element of it.

The family has shape. Order the stable matchings by what the left side wants, writing one matching below another when every left name ranks its partner at least as high. Under this order the family is a **lattice**: any two stable matchings have a stable meet and a stable join.

Knuth 1976 placed that lattice in the theory, following Conway's observation. Its two ends are exactly the matchings the algorithm produces when each side proposes in turn.

## 3 · The mathematics: termination, stability, and the two extremes

**Gale–Shapley 1962.** Run deferred acceptance to its end. The process stops, and the matching it stops on has no blocking pair.

Termination is a counting argument you can point at in the panel. Each proposer walks down its own list and never proposes to the same receiver twice, so the total number of proposals is at most $|L| \times |R|$, and a bounded supply of proposals cannot fuel an endless loop.

Stability is an exchange argument on the final round. Suppose some left name $a$ prefers right name $w$ to its final partner, and $w$ in turn prefers $a$ to its own. Then $a$ proposed to $w$ before the run ended, and $w$ must have turned $a$ down for someone it ranks higher; that someone is $w$'s final partner, contradicting the assumption.

**Proposer optimality (Gale–Shapley 1962; Dubins–Freedman 1981).** The matching that deferred acceptance returns is the left side's favourite: no stable matching gives any left name a partner it prefers to the one it gets.

That is why the panel labels its two directions "optimal for left" and "optimal for right". Swap who proposes and the algorithm walks the lattice to the opposite end, so the two runs together expose both extremes.

## 4 · The centrepiece: drive the board from both ends

Go back to the board and switch the proposing side. The panel recomputes from the start, and the two runs land on the two extremes: left-optimal A–w, B–x, C–z, D–y, and right-optimal A–x, B–w, C–z, D–y.

Read the blocking grid under a finished run. Each cell is the final match, a pair that would block, a case where one side already holds someone it ranks higher, or a pair that is not mutually acceptable; the count of blocking pairs is printed below the grid and it is zero. The extremes table puts both partners side by side and marks the two positions where the extremes disagree.

The grid is only meaningful once the run has finished, because until then the matching is provisional. Step through the early rounds and the blocking count is positive; it falls to zero exactly at the last round.

Switch the board to augmenting paths and the question changes. Kuhn 1955 grows a matching by finding an alternating path that begins and ends at unmatched names and flipping it, which is Berge's criterion 1957: a matching is maximum exactly when no such path exists.

Here every left name accepts every right name, so a matching of size four is available and the search reaches it. Notice what that mode does *not* claim: it maximises the number of pairs and says nothing about stability, so a maximum matching can be riddled with blocking pairs.

## 5 · The payoff

The medical residency match is deferred acceptance at national scale. The National Resident Matching Program assigns graduates to hospitals every year, and its redesign is why the algorithm sits at the centre of the labour-market literature (Roth 1984).

School choice, course assignment and kidney exchange reuse the same skeleton. Each project chooses its variant for what the mechanism must guarantee, which is usually a statement about who gets the best end of the lattice.

## 6 · Optional: what the mechanism does not promise

Skippable. Deferred acceptance is strategy-proof for the proposing side: no proposer can improve its own outcome by misreporting its list (Dubins–Freedman 1981). Receivers carry no such guarantee, and the same instance can be gamed from the other direction.

The set of names matched at all is the same in every stable matching, a fact known as the rural hospitals theorem (Roth 1986); only the partners move from one extreme to the other. When one side's preference lists are all identical, the lattice collapses to a single matching.
