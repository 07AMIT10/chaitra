## Six Atoms on a Table

Relabelling the block you merge into is the obvious implementation, and at $n = 10^9$ it writes $10^{18}$ labels. At a billion writes a second that is 31.7 years. Union by rank with path compression walks $4 \times 10^9$ parent pointers and finishes in 4 seconds.

Six isolated elements sit on the table in front of you. Press **merge**, and two of them collapse into a single bonded clump that can never be pulled apart.

The picture is **the lattice**: nodes are the partitions of the set, cover edges are single merges, and the climb only goes up.

## The Trap of Storing Answers

Fix a finite set `S` of `n` elements. You will be asked, over and over, two questions: are **x** and **y** in the same block, and can you merge the block of **x** with the block of **y**. A first course calls this "disjoint sets" and moves on.

The naive answers are worse than they look. Keep a label array and relabel every member of the block you merge **into** — typically the larger one: one merge can cost Θ(n), so a sequence of `n` merges costs Θ(n²).

Keep a member list per set and copy the longer list on each merge: the same trap, the same quadratic. Relabelling the *smaller* block instead is a real repair and costs O(n log n) over `n` merges, but it still keeps a label per element rather than the partition. Neither method is slow because the idea is bad; both are slow because they store the answer instead of the partition.

## The Lattice You Cannot Descend

The state is a **partition** π of `S`: a set of non-empty, pairwise disjoint blocks whose union is `S`. **find** reads the block containing an element; **merge** fuses two blocks. Nothing else is ever stored.

Partitions of a fixed set are partially ordered by **refinement**: π refines σ when every block of π lies inside a block of σ.

$$
\pi \le \sigma \iff \text{every block of } \pi \text{ lies inside a block of } \sigma
$$

Under that order the partitions form a lattice, the **partition lattice** $\Pi_n$, which is graded and semimodular (Birkhoff, *Lattice Theory*, 1940). The bottom $\bot$ is all singletons, the top $\top$ is the single block `S`, the meet is the common refinement, and the join $\pi \vee \sigma$ has as blocks the connected components of the graph joining elements that share a block in π or in σ.

A **merge(a, b)** is not a new operation. It is the join with the two-block partition that puts **a** and **b** together:

$$
\pi \;\longmapsto\; \pi \vee \pi_{ab}, \qquad \pi_{ab}=\{\{a,b\}\}\cup\{\{x\}: x \ne a,b\}
$$

Only two blocks move, so the join is just "fuse block(a) with block(b)". And because a join can only fuse, **every run of merges is a monotone climb up the lattice** — it can never come back down. Since the two-block partition $\pi_{ab}$ has rank 1, joining with it either fuses two blocks, a single cover step, or fuses nothing, when **a** and **b** already share a block.

Now the number you would have written into the code as a counter:

$$
\rho(\pi) \;=\; n - |\pi| \;=\; \text{blocks fused away} \;=\; \text{successful unions}
$$

The lattice rank and the merge counter are the same number. A runtime statistic is a coordinate of where the state sits in an order-theoretic diagram.

Watch the gold path, not the drawing: when you click a pair from different blocks, one cover edge is taken and the block count falls by exactly one. That fall is the invariant, and it is the same quantity as the merge counter.

## The Physicist's Bank Account

Two facts carry the page.

**Fact 1 — equivalence relations are partitions.** A relation on `S` that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive splits `S` into its classes, and every partition of `S` arises from exactly one such relation. That correspondence is exact: the fibres of **find** are the blocks. Union–Find is not computing connectivity, it is *representing an equivalence relation* and taking its join with the relation $a \sim b$.

**Fact 2 — the physicist's potential method.** Give the state a non-negative number $\Phi$, computable from the state, that you are allowed to spend. If operation $t$ costs $c_t$ real units of work and moves the balance by $\Delta \Phi_t$, call $\hat c_t = c_t + \Delta \Phi_t$ its **amortised** cost. Because the balance never goes below zero,

$$
\sum_t c_t \;=\; \sum_t \hat c_t \;-\; \Phi_{\text{end}} \;+\; \Phi_0 \;\le\; \sum_t \hat c_t \;+\; \Phi_0
$$

When $\Phi_0 = 0$ the statement is the clean one: total real work is at most total amortised work. The balance *is* the proof. You do not have to trust a lemma that lives off-screen; you have to watch a line that never dips below zero.

Two balances do the engineering work, and they fund different things.

- **Rank potential** $\Phi_R = \sum_x \operatorname{rank}(x)$: sum the ranks over all nodes, freezing a node's rank once it stops being a root.
- A merge of two *unequal* ranks changes nothing. A merge of two *equal* ranks raises one root's rank by one, so $\Delta\Phi = +1$, a deposit.
- That deposit turns an actual cost of 1 into an amortised cost of at most 2. Ranks never fall and a root of rank $r$ heads a tree of at least $2^r$ nodes, so the height obeys $h \le \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$.
- The rank balance funds the *merges* and caps the *height*. It does nothing for a find that walks a long path twice.

- **Doubling budget** $\Phi_D = \sum_x \bigl(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor - \operatorname{spent}(x)\bigr)$: charge **x** once each time its parent pointer changes.
- Between two charges the subtree headed by **x**'s parent has at least *doubled*. A node stops being a direct child of a root only when its whole tree is attached beneath a tree at least as large.
- Subtree sizes never exceed `n`, so no node is charged more than $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$ times, and $\Phi_D \ge 0$.
- The doubling balance funds the *finds*, at amortised $O(\log n)$.

Neither balance alone gives the final bound. Ascending from node sizes to their *levels* $L(x) = \log_2^{*}\,\lvert \text{subtree}(x) \rvert$ is what composes them, because a node can be re-hung many times without its level rising, and a level that does rise climbs the iterated logarithm, which has only $\alpha(n)$ rungs.

## Two Repairs for Two Separate Demons

Union by rank or size attacks **height**. Path compression attacks **re-walking the same path**. They are not two ways of saying "make it fast"; running one without the other leaves a different term standing.

$$
\underbrace{O(\log n)}_{\text{rank/size alone}} \qquad \underbrace{O(\log n)}_{\text{compression alone}} \qquad \underbrace{O(\alpha(n))}_{\text{both (Tarjan 1975)}}
$$

The bound is the **inverse Ackermann function**. Build the Ackermann hierarchy

$$
A_0(j)=j+1,\quad A_1(j)=2j+1,\quad A_2(j)=2^{\,j+1}-1,\quad\text{and for } k\ge 3,\quad A_k(j)=A_{k-1}^{(j+1)}(j)
$$

and define $\alpha(n) = \min\{\,k : A_k(1) \ge n\,\}$. Then $A_3(1) = 15$, and $A_4(1) = A_3(15)$ is a power tower of twos about twenty storeys high.

Every set you will ever hold has fewer than $A_4(1)$ elements, so $\alpha(n) \le 4$ throughout. In particular $\alpha(10^{80}) = 4$, and $10^{80}$ already exceeds the number of atoms in the observable universe. Reaching $\alpha = 5$ needs $n > A_4(1)$, a number no computer can write down.

Do not confuse $\alpha$ with the iterated logarithm. The iterated logarithm $\log_2^{*} n = \min\{\,k : 2 \uparrow\uparrow k \ge n\,\}$ ticks once per *doubling of a tower*, and $\log_2^{*}(10^{80}) = 5$ while $\alpha(10^{80}) = 4$. The two functions are constantly conflated; the table keeps them in separate columns for that reason.

The bound is tight, not merely an upper bound. A class of algorithms needs $\Omega(m\,\alpha(m,n))$ time (Tarjan 1979), and in the cell-probe model the lower bound holds with no restrictions (Fredman and Saks 1989).

So Union–Find is genuinely not linear. It is the slowest function you will ever actually meet.

The step from the doubling budget to $\alpha(n)$ is the one place this page cites instead of derives. Composing the two balances into $O(m\,\alpha(n))$ is an intricate level argument, and the honest response to an intricate step is to attribute it and show you where the cost goes, not to wave at a proof.

## When Connectivity Costs Nothing

Kruskal's algorithm sorts the `E` edges once, at cost $O(E \log E)$, then asks for each edge in that order whether its two endpoints are already in one block (Kruskal 1956). The question is **find** and accepting the edge is **merge**. With both repairs the second phase costs $O(E\,\alpha(V))$, so it vanishes into the sort:

$$
O(E \log E) \;+\; O(E\,\alpha(V)) \;=\; O(E \log E)
$$

The same predicate answers image segmentation, where union–find is literally the inner loop of a greedy region merge (Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher 2004), and cluster labelling, where you sweep the open sites of a lattice, merge neighbours, and ask whether two boundaries fell into one block (Hoshen and Kopelman 1976). The partition does the logic; the sort does the cost.
